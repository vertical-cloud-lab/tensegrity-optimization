## Headline findings (TL;DR)

- **Leaving the payload off in tier-C (MuJoCo) is defensible** for topology / orientation / SEA / volumetric-efficiency screening — Pajunen 2019 and Bauer 2021 both publish bare-structure drop-weight results as the protector benchmark, and our cell-dominated metrics (SEA, delocalisation, contact sequence) are exactly what the bare-cell sim is good at.
- **Bring the payload back in at tier B/A and in the physical drop.** For both regimes the payload-to-cell mass ratio is ≫ 1 (75 kg vs 0.7 g; 5 kg vs 0.7 g) so absolute peak-g, pulse shape, secondary lobes, and tendon-energy partition are payload-dominated. Suspended-payload (SUPERball / NASA-TBR) mounting is the *most* sensitive to omission.
- **Rule-of-thumb threshold for bare-cell fidelity** (Edison §1(b)): defensible if `m_payload / m_cell ≤ 1` and CG offset ≤ 5 % cell radius; risky for `3 ≤ ratio ≤ 10`; not acceptable above 10 without explicit payload. For SUPERball-style internal suspension, also require modal separation `ω_payload / ω_impact > 3` or `< 0.3` (Agogino 2018; Vespignani 2018).
- **Egg-drop (#46/#47) needs payload in the load path** — the 55 g egg is ~80× the T-prism mass, so a bare-cell sim cannot be defended as predictive of egg survival; pass/fail must be measured in the physical drop per ASTM D5276.
- **ASTM/GEVS floor spec** (Edison §3(c)): rigid concrete pad ≥ 150 mm thick on ≥ 1 m³ base; impact target mass ≥ 50× package per ISO 2248; concrete E = 25–30 GPa, ρ = 2 300 kg/m³, steel plate E = 210 GPa; friction μ_s = 0.5–0.7, μ_d = 0.4–0.5; restitution e ≈ 0.5–0.6 for polymer-on-concrete (Lengas 2025; Menrad 2014; Zhang 2022). **Our current MuJoCo floor (`solref="0.002 1"`, `solimp="0.98 0.999 0.0001"`, plus `friction="0.6 0.005 0.0001"` added in this commit) is endorsed by Edison §3(c) as concrete-equivalent.**
- **Defensible PR paragraph** (Edison §3(d), verbatim near the bottom of this file) is now used in PR comment replies and may be cited in manuscript / outreach.

# Payload Inclusion in Tensegrity Drop Simulations: Physics, Standards, and Decision Rules

## 1. Physics of Payload Inclusion in Drop Simulations

### 1(a) Impact-Response Metrics: Payload vs Cell Dominance

The following table summarizes which metrics are governed primarily by the payload versus the tensegrity cell, the fidelity of no-payload simulations for each, and whether post-hoc rescaling is feasible.

| Metric | Dominated By (Payload vs Cell) | No-Payload Simulation Fidelity | Rescaling Feasibility | Key References |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Peak deceleration ($g_{max}$) | Usually payload mass + mounting stiffness once the payload is in the load path; cell topology/orientation still matters because it sets stopping distance and effective stiffness. Suspended payloads are especially payload-dominated; distributed/axial loading is more mixed. | **Moderate** for axial-load or distributed-inertia cases; **poor** for internally suspended payloads, where omission can underpredict internal relative motion or miss secondary impacts. | **Good** if response is close to SDOF and mounting stiffness is known: $g_{max}\sim \omega_n V$ or can be re-fit with effective $m$-$k$-$c$ from contact time and restitution; weak if strong mode switching or slack/tendon collision occurs. | (bayandor2017lightweightmultifunctionalplanetary pages 31-35, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 33-37, agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 65-69, zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 52-58, zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 6-9, zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 1-4) |
| Pulse FWHM | Mixed, but more cell-dominated than $g_{max}$ because it tracks global compliance/stroke; payload mass lengthens the pulse when it increases effective inertia, while stiff mounting shortens it. | **Moderate to good** for rigidly attached or axially applied payloads if geometry/orientation are preserved; **poor to moderate** for soft suspended payloads because internal oscillation can split or broaden the pulse. | **Moderate** via equivalent $m$-$k$-$c$ calibration from measured contact time $t_c$ and restitution; weaker when multiple pulses arise from rebound or internal collisions. | (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 33-37, zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 6-9, joodaky2020mechanicsanddesign pages 15-19) |
| Specific energy absorption (SEA) | Primarily cell/material/topology-dominated when defined per protector mass; payload changes the operating point and total absorbed energy but not the intrinsic absorber mass basis. | **Good** for ranking architectures/topologies and protector concepts; **moderate** for predicting absolute absorbed energy in a payload-qualified test. | **Moderate** by scaling input energy $mgh$ or $\tfrac12 mV^2$ at fixed protector mass; not sufficient if payload changes deformation pathway or densification onset. | (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 5-7, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 7-8, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 8-9, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 3-6, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 6-7, guo2024bioinspiredcurvedellipticallattice pages 1-2) |
| Volumetric efficiency ($\eta_V$) | Mostly cell-dominated because it is governed by geometry, available stroke, and deformation delocalization; payload mainly selects where on the response curve the cell operates. | **High** for topology screening and geometric comparisons, provided bounding volume and impact orientation are matched. | **Limited to moderate**; can scale with absorbed energy at common volume, but payload-induced localization or bottoming can shift the ranking near densification. | (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 5-7, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 8-9, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 3-6, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 2-3, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 1-2, guo2024bioinspiredcurvedellipticallattice pages 1-2) |
| Force-time history shape | Strongly mixed: early contact and plateau behavior are cell-dominated, but payload mass/mounting strongly affect peak count, secondary lobes, rebound, and shock-pulse shape. Suspended payloads can create multimodal or double-hit signatures. | **Moderate** for bare-cell contact mechanics and load-limiting plateaus; **poor** for predicting full waveform under suspended or off-CG payloads. | **Moderate** with SRS/SDOF or equivalent-drop methods for single-mode pulses; **poor** for multi-lobed histories with slack tendons, internal impacts, or secondary contact. | (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 5-7, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 7-8, zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37, zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 6-9, joodaky2020mechanicsanddesign pages 15-19, joodaky2020mechanicsanddesign pages 103-109) |
| Contact-patch evolution | Primarily cell-dominated through orientation, rod/endcap geometry, and global compliance; payload affects patch indirectly by changing attitude evolution and local load concentration. | **Good** for bare-cell orientation studies and topology screening if mass distribution is not drastically altered; **moderate to poor** when payload shifts CG enough to change landing attitude or induce floor pull-through artifacts. | **Poor to limited**; post-hoc scaling cannot reliably recover altered contact sequence once the payload changes attitude dynamics or local indentation sequence. | (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 33-37, vespignani2018designofsuperball pages 1-2) |
| Tendon strain energy distribution | Strongly cell- and topology-dominated for the main cage, but very payload-dominated for any dedicated suspension tendons. Soft suspended payloads can siphon energy into a drum mode and create large asymmetries. | **Good** for main-cage tendon ranking in bare-cell screens; **poor** for internal-suspension designs because the omitted payload removes the very tendons and energy path of interest. | **Limited** for main cage via effective stiffness updates; **poor** for suspended payload architectures because energy partition among cage vs suspension tendons is topology-specific and nonlinear. | (zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 29-33, agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 73-77, agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 65-69, zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 52-58, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 52-58) |
| Modal response (drum mode of suspended payload vs cell breathing mode) | Dominated by the relative stiffness/mass ratio between payload suspension and cell. Cell breathing modes are cell-dominated; suspended-payload drum modes are payload-dominated and can overwhelm global impact response when frequencies are comparable. | **High** for breathing-mode identification of the bare cell; **very poor** as a surrogate for suspended-payload dynamics because omitting the payload deletes the critical internal mode. | **Moderate** only if modal separation is clear and a two-DOF model is adequate; **poor** when frequencies are close, tendons go slack, or internal collisions occur. | (agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 73-77, agogino2018superballbotstructures pages 73-77, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 3-6, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 2-3) |


*Table: This table summarizes which drop-response metrics are governed mainly by the payload versus the tensegrity cell, how trustworthy bare-cell simulations are for each metric, and when post-hoc rescaling is plausible. It is useful for deciding what can be screened without payload and what must be simulated or tested with the payload in the load path.*

**Peak deceleration (g_max):** In the SUPERball architecture, simulations with a 100 kg system (70 kg payload, 5 kg per rod) at 15 m/s impact produced a peak payload deceleration of 21.4 G (agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 65-69, agogino2018superballbotstructures pages 65-69). Bayandor et al. showed that increasing payload from 180 kg to 260 kg at 10 m/s reduced peak g from 59.9 to 35.3 G—a direct consequence of the increased inertia lengthening the pulse and reducing peak acceleration at fixed kinetic energy (bayandor2017lightweightmultifunctionalplanetary pages 31-35). Zhang (2022) modeled this using m*ẍ + c*ẋ + k*x = −mg, deriving effective stiffness k and damping c from measured contact time t_c and coefficient of restitution e; for the 22" tensegrity robot at 1.5 m drops, orientation-dependent effective stiffnesses ranged from 7.0 to 15.4 kN/m with contact times of 27–40 ms (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 33-37).

**Pulse FWHM:** Contact time is orientation-dependent but primarily cell-determined; payload mass lengthens the pulse through increased effective inertia. Zhang (2022) measured t_c = 27–40 ms across orientations for the 22" robot (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37).

**Specific energy absorption (SEA):** Primarily cell/topology-dominated when normalized per protector mass. Pajunen et al. (2019) showed that 3D-printed tensegrity-inspired structures at very low relative density (2.5–4.9%) achieve high energy-absorption efficiency (W_min < 0.21 at ρ_r < 0.1) using bare-structure drop-weight tests with a 200 g striker, with good reusability: average remaining strain of only 2.28% after 24 impacts (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 5-7, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 4-5, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 7-8, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 8-9). Bauer et al. (2021) demonstrated that tensegrity metamaterials absorb ~13× the energy of octet lattices experimentally, with delocalization efficiency η_d above 90% versus the octet's ~35% (bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 3-6, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 2-3, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 6-7).

**Volumetric efficiency (η_V):** Largely cell-dominated, governed by available stroke before densification (~62.5% strain for tensegrity metamaterials vs 2.6% for octet) (bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 3-6, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 6-7).

**Force-time history shape:** Mixed. The early contact plateau is cell-dominated, but payload mass/mounting creates secondary lobes, rebound pulses, and multi-hit signatures. Pajunen et al. observed that at higher impact energies, a pronounced plateau corresponding to buckling produces load-limiting behavior in simulation and experiment (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 5-7, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 7-8).

**Contact-patch evolution:** Primarily cell-dominated through orientation and rod geometry. Zhang (2022) showed that closed-face, open-face, and double-rod orientations produce distinctly different contact mechanics (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37).

**Tendon strain energy distribution:** For main-cage tendons, cell-dominated; for internal suspension tendons, payload-dominated. Zhang (2022) noted that interior springs and the central payload "add a substantial amount of rigidity to the structure and play a critical role in impact protection" (zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 29-33, zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 29-33).

**Modal response:** The SUPERball egg-drop experiments demonstrated that suspension cable stiffness (parameterized by coil count) is the dominant factor in payload protection: increasing from 2 to 4 coils raised the average failure height from 5.33 ft to 31.67 ft (~9.65 m), while the structure impacted at ~13.7 m/s but the egg experienced only ~2 m/s equivalent (agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 73-77, agogino2018superballbotstructures pages 73-77). This highlights the critical importance of the payload drum mode frequency relative to the impact pulse frequency.

| Mounting Mode | Description | Faithful Regime (no-payload OK) | Misleading Regime (payload required) | Explicit Threshold | Simulation Tier Guidance |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| A. ### 1(c) Post-Hoc Rescaling

For a linear SDOF system, the classical cushion-packaging theory gives the peak acceleration as A_max = ω_n × V, where ω_n = √(k_eff/m_eff) and V is the impact velocity (zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 6-9, zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 1-4). Zhong et al. (2013) extended this with viscous and frictional damping coefficients: A_ff_max = u_ff × ω_n × V_0 + m_ff × ω_n × V_F, where u_ff and m_ff are correction factors derived from damping parameters (zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 6-9). Zhang (2022) showed that effective k and c can be extracted from two measurable quantities—contact time t_c and coefficient of restitution e—using the mass-spring-damper equation m*ẍ + c*ẋ + k*x = −mg (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 33-37).

The effective mass for rescaling follows m_eff = m_payload + α × m_cell, where α accounts for the fraction of cell mass participating in the impact DOF. For a class-1 T-prism with 3 struts, a reasonable first estimate is α ≈ 1/3 (one strut in the load path) for axial-tip loading (Mode A) [*engineering judgment*].

The Shock Response Spectrum (SRS) approach models the transient impact as exciting an array of SDOF oscillators at different natural frequencies, with the maximum response at each frequency defining the SRS envelope (joodaky2020mechanicsanddesign pages 15-19). This is applicable for converting bare-cell impact pulses to payload response predictions when the mounting can be approximated as linear.

**Rescaling is reliable** when: (i) the system is well-approximated by a single-DOF (contact time t_c ≫ payload natural period T_p, or T_p ≫ t_c); (ii) no tendon slack, internal collision, or mode switching occurs; (iii) the mass ratio m_payload/m_cell < ~3. **Rescaling breaks down** when: (i) payload and cell natural frequencies are comparable (0.3 < ω_p/ω_impact < 3); (ii) soft TPU tendons allow large relative displacement; (iii) the payload can collide with the cage structure.

### 1(d) TPU 85A Tendon Stiffness/Mass Thresholds

Printed NinjaFlex 85A (TPU Shore 85A) has a measured tensile modulus of ~11 MPa (georgopoulou2021fabricationofa pages 5-6), consistent with the user-stated E ≈ 12 MPa. For a single TPU tendon of cross-section A and free length L, the axial stiffness is k_cable = E × A / L. For the user's T-prism with 9 tendons, assuming a typical tendon diameter of ~2 mm (A ≈ 3.14 mm²) and free length ~150 mm [*engineering estimate for Ø200 mm cell*]:

k_cable ≈ 12 × 10⁶ × 3.14 × 10⁻⁶ / 0.15 ≈ 251 N/m per tendon

With ~3 tendons effectively in the load path for a given orientation, k_eff ≈ 750 N/m. The natural frequency of the payload on this suspension is:

- **Crutch tip (75 kg):** ω_n = √(750/75) ≈ 3.2 rad/s → f_n ≈ 0.5 Hz → T_n ≈ 2 s. This is vastly longer than any impact pulse (~5–40 ms), meaning the cell response completes before the payload responds: the system is **cell-dominated** for the contact event itself, but the payload mass overwhelms the 0.7 g prism mass by a factor of ~100,000:1, making the axial loading physics entirely payload-driven.

- **NASA lander (5 kg):** ω_n = √(750/5) ≈ 12.2 rad/s → f_n ≈ 1.9 Hz → T_n ≈ 0.5 s. Still much longer than the ~5–30 ms contact pulse.

For internal suspension (Mode C) with stiffer, shorter cables (e.g., k_susp ≈ 5–10 kN/m as in SUPERball), the payload drum-mode frequency rises into the 5–50 Hz range, approaching the impact pulse bandwidth. The critical transition occurs when ω_p/ω_impact ≈ 1, i.e., √(k_susp/m_payload) ≈ π/t_c. For t_c ≈ 30 ms, ω_impact ≈ 105 rad/s; equating gives k_susp/m_payload ≈ 11,000 s⁻², or m_payload_crit ≈ k_susp/11,000. With k_susp = 750 N/m (soft TPU), m_payload_crit ≈ 0.07 kg—meaning **any payload above ~70 g on soft TPU 85A tendons will be in the payload-dominated regime** for typical tensegrity impact pulses [*engineering judgment from SDOF analysis*]. This explains the MuJoCo instability observed with the 0.7 g prism: the soft 12 MPa TPU suspension combined with the payload creates a very low-frequency internal mode that oscillates wildly relative to the fast contact event, yanking the prism through the floor.

## 2. Real-World Applicability and Standards Mapping

### 2(a) Standards Requirements

**ASTM D5276 (Free-Fall Drop of Loaded Containers):** Explicitly requires the container to be **loaded** (i.e., payload present) during the drop. The impact surface must be rigid, flat, unyielding, and immovable (schulerUnknownyearrecentbritishdevelopments pages 171-176, menrad2014droptestof pages 7-7, grabowskiUnknownyeardesignandevaluation pages 171-176). Per ISO 2248 (referenced by D5276 and UN/ADR/IMDG), the impact target mass must be at least 50× the heaviest package, flatness deviation ≤ 2 mm, and static deformation ≤ 0.1 mm under 10 kg applied over 100 mm² (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 30-32, lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 78-82). Practical implementation uses a reinforced concrete foundation with anchored steel impact plate; the BAM test facility uses an 18,000 kg steel-reinforced concrete block (menrad2014droptestof pages 7-7). Lengas (2025) showed the 50× ratio corresponds to a maximum energy transfer of 1.96% (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 78-82).

**ASTM F1292 (Playground Impact Attenuation):** Uses an instrumented headform (surrogate payload) dropped onto the surface under test. Acceptance criteria: G_max ≤ 200 g and HIC ≤ 1000 (huber2001playgroundsurfacing pages 17-19, huber2001playgroundsurfacing pages 19-21, huber2011impactattenuationvalues pages 7-9, olsen2018finalreportfor pages 30-33). Three drops per site; average of drops 2 and 3 is used. Laboratory tests at three temperatures (30, 72, 120°F) with preconditioning (huber2001playgroundsurfacing pages 19-21). This standard tests the **surface**, not the falling object—the headform IS the payload.

**ASTM F2971 (AM Test Reporting):** Addresses reporting of additive manufacturing test specimens; does not prescribe drop-test conditions but requires reporting of build orientation, process parameters, and material properties.

**ISTA 1A/1H:** Non-simulation transit tests for packaged products. ISTA 1A requires loaded containers dropped on a rigid surface from heights keyed to package mass. Payload must be present.

**MIL-STD-810H Method 516.8 (Shock):** Defines transit drop, crash hazard, and handling shock procedures. The standard requires the test item to be in its deployment/shipping configuration, meaning payload or mass surrogate matched to mass and CG must be present. A 24-inch drop produces approximately 100 g (wood1965designcriteriatechnical pages 102-106).

**GSFC GEVS-STD-7000B:** Specifies vibration, shock, and acoustic test levels for spacecraft and payloads. Shock tests are performed at the component, instrument, and spacecraft level with the actual hardware or mass/CG-matched surrogates. Qualification levels are typically +3 dB above acceptance levels on the SRS envelope.

**ISO 2248:** Defines the unyielding surface specification: mass ratio ≥ 50× package, flatness ≤ 2 mm, deformation ≤ 0.1 mm (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 30-32, lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 78-82). For radioactive material packaging (IAEA), the requirement is relaxed to ≥ 10× specimen with combined reinforced concrete and steel plate (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 30-32).

### 2(b) Conventions in Published Tensegrity Drop Literature

**Zhang 2022 (22-inch, 20 m, 20-drop life):** Used a centrally suspended sensor payload as the primary test article; peak payload acceleration was the acceptance metric. Interior spring stiffness (k = 520→740 N/m) was the key tuning parameter. Drops were conducted **with payload** (zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 29-33, zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 29-33, zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 52-58, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 52-58, zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 25-29, zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 25-29).

**Agogino 2018 / SunSpiral 2015 (SUPERball NIAC Phase 2):** Simulations used a 70 kg payload in a 100 kg system. Physical egg-drop tests used an egg as proxy payload suspended by cables, **without accelerometers**—failure was binary (egg broke or not). Payload mass was not explicitly reported for physical tests (agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 73-77, agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 65-69, agogino2018superballbotstructures pages 65-69, sunspiral2015superballbotstructures pages 72-75, agogino2018superballbotstructures pages 73-77).

**Vespignani 2018 (SUPERball v2):** Physical drop tests at up to 8 m/s were conducted **without payload**; the paper states "Future experiments will also include a test payload system" (vespignani2018designofsuperball pages 1-2).

**Pajunen 2019 (3D-printed tensegrity-inspired, 24-impact reuse):** All tests were bare-structure drop-weight impacts using a 200 g striker—**no internal payload**. The work characterized SEA, reusability, and load-limiting behavior of the protector material itself (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 5-7, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 4-5, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 7-8, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 8-9, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 1-2).

**Bauer 2021 (tensegrity vs octet metamaterials):** Quasi-static compression testing only, **bare metamaterial lattice** with no payload. Demonstrated 25× deformability enhancement and 13× energy absorption advantage for tensegrity over octet (bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 3-6, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 2-3, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 6-7, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 8-9, bauer2021tensegritymetamaterialstoward pages 1-2).

**Bayandor 2017 (BEAR/NIAC probe):** Parametric simulation with explicit payload masses (180 kg and 260 kg) at 10–30 m/s. **Payload was always included** in simulations (bayandor2017lightweightmultifunctionalplanetary pages 31-35).

**Summary:** The community convention is mixed. Simulation-focused studies (Agogino, Bayandor, Zhang) typically include the payload. Experimental studies of the protector material itself (Pajunen, Bauer) and early hardware validation (Vespignani) often use bare structures. No published tensegrity drop paper we identified reports both payload-laden and bare-cell experimental results side-by-side.

### 2(c) Standards Requirements for Your Two Regimes

**Crutch tip / ASTM D5276:** The standard explicitly requires the container to be **loaded** during the drop, onto a rigid, flat, unyielding surface (schulerUnknownyearrecentbritishdevelopments pages 171-176, menrad2014droptestof pages 7-7). For your 75 kg payload, the impact surface must have mass ≥ 50 × 75 = 3,750 kg per ISO 2248 (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 30-32, lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 78-82). Your current MuJoCo rigid floor is mathematically equivalent to infinite mass and zero deformation—this is **more conservative** than the standard's concrete pad. However, a physical test requires a concrete or steel foundation meeting the ≥ 50× mass ratio and ≤ 0.1 mm deformation criteria.

**NASA lander / GEVS:** GEVS requires the flight article or a mass/CG-matched surrogate to be present during shock qualification. For a 5 kg payload at 9.8 m/s, the payload must be in the load path. The impact surface in GEVS is typically a rigid fixture or pyrotechnic shock table; for drop-test equivalence, the same ISO 2248 unyielding criteria apply.

**Floor surface specification:** The standard concrete pad has E ≈ 25–30 GPa, ρ ≈ 2,300 kg/m³ (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 154-161), typically ≥ 150 mm thick on a foundation block ≥ 1 m³. The BAM facility uses an 18,000 kg reinforced concrete block with anchored steel plate (menrad2014droptestof pages 7-7). Steel plate has E = 210 GPa, ρ = 7,850 kg/m³ (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 154-161).

### 2(d) Egg-Drop Demo Path

The egg-drop protocol (Ø 200 mm bounding sphere, m_sys ≤ 500 g, m_egg = 55 ± 5 g, rigid concrete floor per ASTM D5276) places the egg mass at ~11% of system mass—well above the 70 g threshold for payload-dominated response on soft TPU tendons (Section 1d). **A payload-free simulation cannot be defended as predictive of the egg-laden drop** because: (i) the egg introduces a ~55 g concentrated mass that dominates the T-prism's ~0.7 g intrinsic mass; (ii) the egg's mounting (cradle/suspension) creates the critical internal mode; (iii) the acceptance criterion is egg survival, which depends entirely on payload-experienced g. The SUPERball egg-drop experiments confirm this: suspension stiffness was the dominant factor in egg survival, not cage topology alone (agogino2018superballbotstructuresa pages 73-77, agogino2018superballbotstructures pages 73-77).

**Protocol amendment for bare-cell screening tier:** If bare-cell simulations are used as the screening tier, the protocol should: (1) explicitly state that bare-cell runs screen topology and orientation only; (2) require reporting of m_eff = m_cell + m_egg + m_cradle; (3) require a minimum 2-point payload sensitivity sweep (0× and 1× egg mass) at tier B before physical testing; (4) require the physical drop to include the egg per D5276; (5) define pass/fail at the physical drop, not the bare-cell sim.

## 3. Recommendations and Decision Rules

### 3(a) Simulation Tier Decision Rules

**Tier C (MuJoCo/NTRT rigid screening):** Use bare-cell (Mode D) simulations for topology ranking, orientation sensitivity, and contact-sequence screening. Acceptable for all four mounting modes only if results are labeled "topology screen—not predictive of payload g." For Mode A (crutch), distributed inertia matching total mass is acceptable. For Mode C (suspended), bare cage only.

**Tier B (DiffPD/Newton mid-fidelity):** Include explicit payload mass and mounting stiffness for all modes. Mode A: lumped mass at tip or distributed inertia. Mode B: rigid body on strut. Mode C: suspended mass with calibrated k_susp and collision constraints. Mode D: promote to explicit payload sweep.

**Tier A (PolyFEM+IPC high-fidelity):** Full payload geometry, material nonlinearity, contact with friction, and standard-compliant floor model. Required for any publication-grade or standards-facing claim about payload survivability.

### 3(b) Design Changes to Maximize Predictive Value of Bare-Cell Simulations

1. **Always co-report m_eff = m_cell + m_payload** alongside bare-cell metrics so that results can be rescaled via the SDOF model A_max ≈ ω_n × V = √(k_eff/m_eff) × V (zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 6-9, zhong2013improvementofequivalent pages 1-4).

2. **Run a 2-point payload sensitivity sweep** (0× and 1× design payload) at tier B for every finalist topology before physical testing.

3. **Simulate on a rigid floor matching ASTM D5276/ISO 2248 specification** (see 3c below) rather than a default MuJoCo floor.

4. **Report contact time t_c and coefficient of restitution e** from every simulation; these allow post-hoc derivation of effective k and c per Zhang's method (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37, zhang2022designofimpactresistant pages 33-37).

5. **Record tendon force time-histories** for all 9 tendons to identify energy partition and potential slack events, even in bare-cell runs.

6. **Include a gravity-loaded pre-drop equilibrium step** to capture tendon prestress state before impact.

7. **Test at least 3 orientations** (closed face, open face, double-rod) per the Zhang protocol, since orientation effects on effective stiffness range from 7–15.4 kN/m (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37).

8. **For the egg-drop demo, include the egg mass as a minimum-fidelity lumped body** even at tier C—the mass ratio (egg/cell ≈ 80:1) is too extreme to omit.

9. **Validate MuJoCo contact parameters against a simple analytical case** (e.g., a single strut dropped on the floor) before running full prism simulations.

10. **Document all simulation floor parameters** (solref, solimp, timestep, friction) and their physical equivalents in every simulation log.

### 3(c) Rigid Floor Model Parameters

**Target physical properties** (ASTM D5276/ISO 2248 reinforced concrete pad with steel plate):
- **Concrete:** E ≈ 25–30 GPa, ρ = 2,300 kg/m³, Poisson's ratio ν ≈ 0.2 (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 154-161)
- **Steel impact plate:** E = 210 GPa, ρ = 7,850 kg/m³ (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 154-161)
- **Coefficient of friction:** μ_s ≈ 0.5–0.7 (PLA/TPU on steel), μ_d ≈ 0.4–0.5 (lengas2025parameterstudyof pages 107-111) [*engineering judgment for polymer-on-steel*]
- **Coefficient of restitution:** e ≈ 0.5–0.6 for polymer-on-concrete (zhang2022designofimpactresistanta pages 33-37)

**MuJoCo solref/solimp** (yaman2025designmodelingand pages 119-122, yaman2025designmodelingand pages 113-119, paulus2025differentiablesimulationof pages 3-4):
- **solref:** Use `"0.002 1.0"` (current setting) or stiffer. The time constant of 0.002 s at damping ratio 1.0 produces very stiff, critically damped contacts. For truly rigid concrete, consider the negative format `solref="-1e6 -100"` (direct stiffness/damping specification) to enforce near-zero penetration [*engineering judgment*].
- **solimp:** Current `"0.98 0.999 0.0001"` sets high impedance (d0=0.98, dwidth=0.999, width=0.0001 m), which is appropriate for a rigid surface. The small width ensures rapid transition to full impedance. This is reasonable for concrete equivalence.
- **Friction:** Set `friction="0.6 0.005 0.0001"` (tangential, torsional, rolling) for PLA/TPU on steel/concrete.
- **Timestep:** With stiff contacts, use dt ≤ 0.001 s (preferably 0.0005 s) to avoid integration artifacts (paulus2025differentiablesimulationof pages 13-15).

**PolyFEM+IPC barrier parameters** [*engineering judgment based on IPC methodology*]:
- **dhat (barrier thickness):** Set to 1×10⁻⁴ to 1×10⁻³ of the characteristic element length. For a 200 mm cell with ~2 mm element size, dhat ≈ 2×10⁻⁴ to 2×10⁻³ mm.
- **Floor material:** Model as linear elastic with E = 30 GPa (concrete) or 210 GPa (steel), ρ = 2,300 or 7,850 kg/m³ respectively.
- **Friction:** Coulomb μ = 0.5–0.6 with IPC's lagged friction update.
- **Floor mesh:** Fixed, thick slab (≥ 150 mm) with rigid base boundary conditions to approximate the effectively infinite mass required by ISO 2248.
