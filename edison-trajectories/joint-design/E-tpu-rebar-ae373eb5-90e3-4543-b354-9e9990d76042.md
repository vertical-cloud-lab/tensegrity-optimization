## Critical feedback on the TPU “rebar” embedded-anchor joint (PETG strut + TPU cable)

### Summary assessment
The embedded-TPU “rebar” concept is directionally sound for PETG–TPU, because published multi-material FDM work repeatedly shows that rigid–flexible polymer interfaces are intrinsically weak and defect-prone unless you introduce macroscopic mechanical interlocking and manage thermal history/sequence (e.g., bed temperature, layer thickness, nozzle height/squeeze, and minimizing dwell between materials) (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2, catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturinga pages 7-11, lopes2024interfaceboundarymechanical pages 37-40). However, the baseline (straight) embed can still fail by interfacial sliding at forces far below what a full-area shear model would predict; your design should assume sliding is the first failure mode unless you add undercuts/barbs and sufficient PETG ligament thickness (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17, catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturinga pages 7-11).

### (1) Manufacturability on a 0.4 mm IDEX printer without soluble support
**What is feasible in one print:**
- **Straight cylindrical embed** is the most manufacturable. It is essentially just a TPU “insert” that PETG overprints/encapsulates. The key risk is not geometric resolution but **interface defects** (voids/air holes) and **weak bonding** at the PETG–TPU boundary due to mismatched melt/viscosity/surface-energy and cooling history (ruwais2025mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-4, catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturinga pages 7-11).
- **Barbed (“Christmas-tree”) embed** is also manufacturable on a 0.4 mm nozzle if barb features respect layer-height quantization. A conservative rule is to keep each barb step height ≥ ~2 layers (e.g., ≥0.4 mm at 0.2 mm layers) so the barb is not just a rounded bead artifact. Mechanical interlocking is repeatedly identified as the effective way to overcome poor chemical compatibility in TPU–rigid polymer pairs (catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturing pages 6-7, zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2).
- **Hook/J embed** is manufacturable only if the PETG cavity that later encapsulates it is **self-supporting** (no “roofing” over open air) and if the hook does not create trapped unsupported volumes. Practically, this is the hardest to print cleanly without support and has the highest variability (lopes2024interfaceboundarymechanical pages 37-40).

**Process constraints that directly matter for your joint:**
- **Bed/platform temperature dominates interface bonding** more than nozzle temperature and print speed in multi-material rigid–flexible joints (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2). Separate work summarized in reviews shows raising build-plate temperature can nearly double (“+93%”) contact strength in TPU/ABS interfaces, indicating that thermal history can overwhelm purely geometric changes (catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturinga pages 7-11).
- **Nozzle height (“squeeze”) matters:** lower nozzle height increases a “squeezing effect” that promotes diffusion/contact and improves bonding; excessive nozzle height degrades bead shape and bonding (lopes2024interfaceboundarymechanical pages 37-40).
- **Material-change dwell/stoppages reduce adhesion:** multi-material printing stoppages/toolchanges can reduce interlayer adhesion and mechanical performance (lopes2024interfaceboundarymechanical pages 37-40). For your design, this translates to: minimize time between printing TPU anchor features and the PETG confinement that locks them.

**Geometric envelope and member convergence:**
- With 6 mm struts, 2.4 mm cables, and ~7 mm node spheres, the limiting factor is not space but **print-path planning** so the PETG has enough continuous perimeters (“ligament”) around each embedded TPU segment to resist shear-out at barbs and to avoid local cracking. Published rigid–flexible interlocking joints show failure can move from interface sliding to fracture of the interlocking teeth/substrate depending on geometry (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17, catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturing pages 6-7).

### (2) Likely failure modes under a single-impact drop test (with quantitative bounds)
Drop-tower loading will impose a mix of axial tendon tension, joint peel/bending, and dynamic amplification. The most probable first failures are joint-dominated (pull-out/sliding, PETG local fracture), not TPU bulk rupture.

**Most likely failure modes (ordered):**
1. **Interfacial sliding / pull-out of the embedded TPU** (dominant for straight embeds). In rigid–flexible FDM joints, alternate-deposition-type interfaces often fail by interlayer sliding; mechanical interlocking reduces sliding but can shift failure to tooth fracture (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17).
2. **PETG “shear-out” / breakout around barbs (tooth-root cracking)** (dominant once you add undercuts). Mechanical interlocking introduces stress concentrations at tooth roots/tips and can cause premature shear fracture of the interlocking features (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17).
3. **Delamination/void-driven crack growth near the PETG–TPU boundary**. Voids and poor interdiffusion are common with mismatched materials and can nucleate cracks (ruwais2025mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-4, catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturinga pages 7-11).
4. **Hinge-like yielding / localized bending in the PETG node** due to the compliant TPU load path creating peel stresses and bending moment at the interface. Flexible polymers can reduce peak stresses but also redistribute deformation; stiff vs flexible joint behavior differs strongly in peel/bending (oz2025acomprehensiveexperimental pages 13-15).
5. **TPU viscoelastic/creep effects** are likely a *secondary* issue for single-impact events (ms-scale), but will matter for pre-tension retention and any sustained loading between impacts.

**Quantitative bounds from published rigid–flexible joint strengths:**
Although direct “TPU cable pull-out from PETG” numbers were not found in the retrieved corpus, multi-material FDM PLA/TPU joint studies provide relevant MPa-level interface strengths and failure modes:
- Mechanical interlocking joints: maximum tensile strength ~6.58 ± 0.33 MPa (at 2 mm depth, 45°) and maximum shear strength ~24.47 ± 1.99 MPa (at 4 mm depth, 22.5°) for PLA/TPU (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2).
- Alternate deposition joints: tensile plateaus ~7.42 ± 0.33 MPa for PLA/TPU at sufficient deposition width, but shear can be limited by sliding (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2, zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17).

**Order-of-magnitude pull-out force estimates for your geometry (Ø2.4 mm, L=8–12 mm):**
- Cylindrical interface area A ≈ π d L ≈ 60–90 mm².
- If you assume an *effective* interfacial shear capacity in the **~10–25 MPa** range (consistent with reported shear stress levels for thermoplastic adhesives and MPa-scale joint strengths in rigid–flexible FDM), the idealized upper bound is **~0.6–2.3 kN** (A·τ) (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2, oz2025acomprehensiveexperimental pages 13-15).
- A more realistic first-pass bound for PETG–TPU, given known voids/weak bonding and documented sliding failures, is often substantially lower; designing around **~0.15–0.45 kN for straight embeds** and **~0.25–0.70 kN for barbed embeds** is a safer starting assumption until you measure it on your machine/materials (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17, catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturinga pages 7-11).

**Why the realistic bound can be far below the idealized bound:**
Interface defects are “more likely” in rigid–flexible multi-material FDM because chemical incompatibility yields inferior bonding and fabrication defects; thermal mismatch stresses during cooling are recognized contributors to interfacial failure (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2). In poorly compatible systems, macroscopic interlocks (T-shapes/teeth) outperform purely “chemical” or low-undercut interfaces, and sliding separation is a common failure mode for non-interlocked geometries (catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturing pages 6-7, zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17).

### (3) Fidelity to the “rigid strut + massless 1‑D tendon” modeling assumption
Your proposed joint will introduce two “simulation-gap” effects:

1. **Non-negligible joint compliance (series spring)**
- TPU is orders of magnitude softer than PETG; TPU modulus values reported for related systems are ~67 MPa (TPU adhesive) while PETG is ~3000 MPa (oz2025acomprehensiveexperimental pages 13-15). Even if your TPU 95A filament is somewhat stiffer than adhesive tests, it remains in the tens-of-MPa regime.
- This means local deformation in the embedded TPU and the immediate PETG–TPU region will add compliance beyond the ideal tendon.

2. **Nonlinear slip / hysteresis (series friction + damage)**
- Published failure-mode comparisons show alternate deposition can fail via PLA–TPU interlayer sliding; mechanical interlocking reduces sliding but adds stress concentration and tooth-root fracture risk (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17). Sliding implies a **Coulomb-like slip element** and hysteresis, which a massless tendon model does not capture.

**Suggested reduced-order joint model for simulation (first pass):**
- Tendon element: axial spring **k_cable = E_TPU·A_cable / L_free**.
- Joint element in series: (a) linear spring representing local embed shear deformation, plus (b) a slip element with static/kinetic thresholds tuned so that gross slip begins near the measured pull-out force (expect 0.1–0.5 kN first pass). The observation that sliding remains a primary bottleneck in poorly compatible joints supports explicitly modeling slip (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17).

### (4) Prior art most closely realizing this concept
**Most relevant quantitative prior art (multi-material FDM rigid–flexible joints):**
- **Zhang et al. 2026** systematically compares **mechanical interlocking vs alternate deposition** in PLA/TPU, reporting tensile strengths ~6.6–7.4 MPa and shear strengths up to ~24.5 MPa with clear failure-mode distinctions: tooth shear fracture vs interlayer sliding (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2, zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17). This is the closest “mechanical interlock in multi-material FDM” analog to your rebar/barb idea.
- **Catana et al. 2024 (review of bicomponent AM)** summarizes experiments indicating PLA/TPU has poor adhesion and that a **macroscopic T-shaped mechanical interlock** outperforms U-shapes/dovetails, with separation otherwise occurring along the interface (sliding) (catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturing pages 6-7, catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturinga pages 7-11).

**Manufacturing/defect mechanism prior art:**
- **Ruwais & Naveed 2025** emphasizes void formation/weak bonding from mismatch in melting temperature/viscosity/surface energy and notes that process optimization can promote mechanical interlocking and improve load transfer (ruwais2025mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-4).
- **Lopes 2024** highlights the effect of nozzle height (“squeeze”) on diffusion/bonding and notes multi-material stoppages can harm interlayer adhesion (lopes2024interfaceboundarymechanical pages 37-40).
- **Öz & Öztürk 2025** provides quantitative context for TPU vs PETG ductility and shear-stress levels (as adhesives), highlighting that flexible phases reduce peak stresses and can improve peel resistance but have low cohesive strength (oz2025acomprehensiveexperimental pages 13-15).

**Additional “go/no-go” recommendations for your first experimental campaign:**
- Start with **straight** and **barbed** anchors; skip J-hooks initially to reduce confounding printability variability (supported by the strong dependence of performance on defects/sequence and on whether failure is sliding vs tooth fracture) (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17, lopes2024interfaceboundarymechanical pages 37-40).
- Use **modifier meshes** (if available) to reduce layer height and speed only in the node/anchor region; literature indicates thinner layers and higher printing temperature improve adhesion, and low nozzle height (squeeze) improves diffusion/contact (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 1-2, lopes2024interfaceboundarymechanical pages 37-40).
- In IDEX scheduling, prioritize: **TPU anchor deposition → immediate PETG capture**, minimizing cooling/dwell; multi-material stoppages degrade adhesion (lopes2024interfaceboundarymechanical pages 37-40).

## Key design critique (most important single point)
If your goal is to preserve “ideal tendons with point attachments,” the straight embed will likely behave as a **progressive-slip anchor** (nonlinear compliance + hysteresis) rather than a rigid attachment. The most simulation-faithful option is therefore a **short embed plus aggressive mechanical undercut (barbs) plus thick PETG ligaments**, so that under peak load the failure mode shifts away from sliding and toward either TPU axial stretch (more “tendon-like”) or PETG bulk response that you can treat as rigid (zhang2026mechanicalperformanceof pages 15-17, catana2024bicomponentadditivemanufacturing pages 6-7).
