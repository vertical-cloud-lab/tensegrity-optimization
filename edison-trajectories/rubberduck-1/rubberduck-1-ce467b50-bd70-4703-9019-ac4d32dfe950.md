# Iteration 1 of 5: Methods and objective stack

## Bottom line

The protocol is operationally credible, but the objective stack does not yet support the physical interpretation assigned to rebound energy. The most serious findings are:

1. **The reported rebound quantity is dimensionally valid but not demonstrably an energy.** If $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ is a velocity-like restitution ratio, energy scales as $e_{\mathrm{reb}}^2$, not $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$. The denominator $\Delta v$ is also the base plate’s velocity change, not a demonstrated incident relative velocity.
2. **The constant-mass account conflicts with the round-2 CSV.** The manuscript says round 2 still uses $m^*=30.95$ g solid mass, but every recommendation has `target_mass_g = 20.23`, predicted printed mass 20.22 g, and solid mass ranging from 25.9340 to 37.0037 g.
3. **The drop-processing pipeline is not reproducible from the Methods or SI.** Numerical impact-window bounds, vector-processing order, second-contact detector, $\Delta v$ integration rule, exclusions, and GP noise propagation are absent and are not marked TBD.
4. **A Results statement is numerically false.** Five, not seven, of the eight tested articles have $t_{180}>1$.
5. **The prior objective review is disclosed faithfully.** The stated Spearman result, ballistic caveat, and 0.72% article-level coefficient of variation agree with the supplied data and SI. The problem is that Section 3.3 still defines the rebound output as physical “energy returned to a payload” before that interpretation has been validated.

## Analysis plan

1. Derive defensible outputs from only the stated sensors and protocol.
2. audit every symbol and physical interpretation in Eq. (1).
3. test numerical claims against the three campaign CSVs.
4. compare the mass projection across Section 3.1, Section 3.4, SI S5, and the recommendation CSV.
5. determine whether the stated GP can consume the described noise and whether SAASBO is justified.

### Probe 1: What objectives follow from the protocol alone?

**Hostile referee:** Before seeing Eq. (1), what per-specimen objectives can you actually derive from the measurements described in Section 3.3 and SI S4?

**Author’s best answer from the materials:** The rig directly supports:

- a filtered top-vertex peak acceleration;
- a filtered top/base peak ratio, provided a peak-search window is specified;
- base-channel $\Delta v$, provided the integration interval and baseline correction are specified;
- pulse duration or impulse-like descriptors from the acceleration records;
- ringdown frequency and, after a stated fit-quality gate, damping ratio;
- within-article repeatability across repeated drops.

The manuscript supports the first, second, and ringdown outputs. It records a single-axis base input and a triaxial top response (main text lines 787–803), and states that ringdown frequency and gated damping ratio are diagnostics rather than objectives (lines 837–840). It also correctly says that crush energy cannot be recovered because there is no falling-mass or displacement channel and the specimens remain elastic (lines 847–868; SI S4 lines 195–201).

The protocol does **not**, by itself, establish a payload rebound energy. It lacks a measured payload mass, rebound displacement, velocity trace, force integral, or validated correspondence between the detected second event and ballistic free flight.

**Verdict: WOUNDED.** Peak shock is a defensible primary objective. Rebound timing is, at present, a candidate descriptor rather than a validated energy objective.

**Concrete edit:** Replace lines 805–840 with language that separates measured metrics from interpreted metrics:

> “Each session yields one primary measured objective, the CFC-180 filtered peak-acceleration ratio $t_{180}$, and one exploratory rebound-timing descriptor $r_t=g t_{\mathrm{second}}/(2\Delta v)$. We do not interpret $r_t$ as a restitution coefficient or convert it to energy until the second event and free-flight assumptions are validated by synchronized video.”

If the existing optimization must be documented historically, call the second quantity the **round-1 provisional rebound score**.

### Probe 2: Is $t_{180}$ mathematically well-defined and reproducible?

**Hostile referee:** What exact samples enter each maximum? Are all three top axes filtered independently before taking the Euclidean norm? Does the norm include pre-impact noise or post-impact rebound? Why is the numerator a vector magnitude while the denominator is a scalar axial channel?

**Author’s best answer from the materials:** Equation (1) defines

$$
t_{180}=\frac{\max_t\lVert\mathbf a_{\mathrm{top}}(t)\rVert_{\mathrm{CFC180}}}{\max_t|a_{\mathrm{base}}(t)|_{\mathrm{CFC180}}},
$$

and the prose says both peaks are taken “within the impact window” (main text lines 808–823). The acquisition record is 100 ms with 2 ms pre-trigger (lines 794–796; SI S4 lines 145–153). CFC-180 is the reporting class and CFC-1000 is diagnostic (lines 796–803). SI S4 also supplies sensor sensitivities and the in-place cross-calibration (SI lines 164–181).

But the attached materials never give:

- numerical impact-window bounds;
- whether each triaxial component is filtered before the norm is formed;
- sensor-axis orientation or any coordinate rotation;
- CFC filter initialization and edge handling;
- whether the top vector includes transverse acceleration by design;
- an exclusion rule for malformed drops.

The vector-over-scalar choice can be legitimate if the objective is total acceleration magnitude at the top and axial base excitation at the carriage, but that intent must be stated. It can also inflate the ratio relative to an axial-to-axial comparison.

**Verdict: FAILS.** The quantity has a formula but not a reproducible algorithm.

**Concrete edit:** Add an algorithm paragraph to Section 3.3 or SI S4 containing, at minimum:

> “Each top-axis record is calibrated and CFC-180 filtered independently before computing $a_{\mathrm{top,mag}}(t)=\sqrt{a_x^2+a_y^2+a_z^2}$. Peaks are searched over $[t_0+\underline{\hspace{0.7cm}},t_0+\underline{\hspace{0.7cm}}]$ ms, where $t_0$ is \underline{trigger/first-contact definition}. The denominator is the absolute peak of the axial base channel over the same window. Filter initialization uses \underline{method}; no padding/\underline{padding method} is used. Drops are excluded only when \underline{predeclared criterion}.”

Fill the blanks from the processing code. Do not leave this as an unmarked omission.

### Probe 4: Is $e_{\mathrm{reb}}=g t_{\mathrm{second}}/(2\Delta v)$ a restitution-like fraction?

**Hostile referee:** Show the mechanics. What velocity is in the numerator, what velocity is in the denominator, and what collision pair defines restitution?

**Author’s best answer from the materials:** Under ideal ballistic flight lasting $t_{\mathrm{second}}$, the upward launch speed would be $v_+=g t_{\mathrm{second}}/2$. Therefore the equation is dimensionless and equals $v_+/\Delta v$. The CSV is internally consistent with this computation. For example, article 6lhxfy has $t_{\mathrm{second}}=55.1786$ ms and $\Delta v=5.3720$ m/s, producing $e_{\mathrm{reb}}=0.05038$ (`t3-prism-bo-batch-drop-results.csv`, row 2).

That does not make it a coefficient of restitution. A restitution coefficient requires a relative separation speed divided by a relative approach speed for a specified collision pair. The denominator here is “the measured base velocity change” (main text lines 830–832), not a demonstrated pre-impact relative speed of the top vertex or payload. The second event is also not yet validated as re-contact after unconstrained ballistic flight. The Discussion admits exactly this at lines 1265–1268.

The cited Bernstein paper is titled “Listening to the coefficient of restitution” (`references.bib` lines 916–925), but a citation to a timing method does not validate this fixture’s collision interpretation.

**Verdict: FAILS.** It is a dimensionless rebound-timing score, not yet a restitution-like fraction with established mechanics.

**Concrete edit:** Rename $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ to $r_t$ or $s_{\mathrm{reb}}$ throughout and replace lines 826–835 with:

> “The provisional rebound-timing score $r_t=g t_{\mathrm{second}}/(2\Delta v)$ is dimensionless. Under an unvalidated free-flight interpretation, its numerator is the launch speed inferred from the interval to a putative second contact. Because $\Delta v$ is a base-channel velocity change rather than a demonstrated incident relative velocity, we do not call $r_t$ a coefficient of restitution.”

### Probe 5: Is $E_{\mathrm{reb}}=e_{\mathrm{reb}}mgh$ physically coherent?

**Hostile referee:** If $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ is a velocity ratio, why is energy linear in $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ rather than quadratic? Why is $h=1.524$ m, the tower drop height, used as the rebound-energy height? Why is $m$ the article mass when the prose claims energy returned to a payload?

**Author’s best answer from the materials:** The expression is dimensionally an energy. It also reproduces the table. Using $h=1.524$ m, article 6lhxfy’s $m=18.50$ g and $e_{\mathrm{reb}}=0.050376$ gives 13.93 mJ, matching the reported 13.9 mJ.

The physical interpretation does not follow. If $e_{\mathrm{reb}}=v_+/v_-$ were a conventional velocity restitution coefficient, the returned kinetic-energy fraction would be $e_{\mathrm{reb}}^2$, and the corresponding value for 6lhxfy would be 0.702 mJ, not 13.93 mJ. More directly, the ballistic speed inferred from $t_{\mathrm{second}}$ gives

$$
E_{\mathrm{ballistic}}=\tfrac12 m\left(\frac{g t_{\mathrm{second}}}{2}\right)^2,
$$

subject to identifying the moving mass. Equation (1) instead multiplies the timing ratio by the gravitational potential $mgh$ associated with the full 1.524 m tower height. The manuscript supplies no derivation connecting those quantities.

The mass identity is also inconsistent. Lines 826–833 call this energy “returned to a payload riding the top vertex,” but $m$ is “each article’s own weighed mass.” The top accelerometer or payload mass is not reported.

**Verdict: FAILS.** The numbers are reproducible from the CSV, but the label “rebound energy returned to a payload” is not mechanically supported.

**Concrete edit:** Delete $E_{\mathrm{reb}}=e_{\mathrm{reb}}mgh$ as a physical energy. For the historical round-1 analysis, use:

> “$S_{\mathrm{reb}}=r_t mgh$ is the provisional mass-weighted rebound score used by the round-1 optimizer. It has energy units but is not interpreted as rebound energy.”

Preferably rerun the surrogate with $r_t$ as a candidate constraint, as already promised in the Discussion. If an energy is retained, define the moving payload mass and derive it from measured or video-validated rebound velocity/height.

### Probe 10: Does the stated noise model define quantities a GP can consume?

**Hostile referee:** A GP consumes an observation variance, not prose. What exact variance did you pass for each objective, and why is print-to-print mass scatter observation noise after you already measured each article’s mass?

**Author’s best answer from the materials:** In principle, yes. A fixed-noise GP can consume one variance per observation. Section 3.4 says it receives the per-drop standard error of the 99-drop mean, with 0.457 g print-to-print mass scatter folded into rebound-energy noise (lines 932–952).

The implementation is not specified. From the supplied article SDs, $t_{180}$ per-drop SEMs would be approximately 0.00017–0.00051 if computed as $s/\sqrt{99}$. A 0.72% article-level floor is approximately 0.0064–0.0076, with a median floor-to-SEM ratio of 21.8 across the eight rows. Thus “roughly an order of magnitude” is conservative, but the actual variance ratio is much larger because variance squares the scale ratio.

The 0.457 g mass scatter corresponds to about 2.1–2.5% of article mass across the mapped results. No equation says whether its energy contribution was propagated as

$$
\sigma^2_E=\sigma^2_{E,\mathrm{drops}}+\left(\frac{\partial E}{\partial m}\sigma_m\right)^2,
$$

or by another method. More fundamentally, measured mass is part of the defined response $E$; article-to-article mass variation is process variability, not scale-measurement error. Treating it as response noise may be reasonable for future replicated builds at the same geometry, but that modeling target must be stated.

**Verdict: FAILS.** The prose names ingredients but does not define the variance supplied to the model.

**Concrete edit:** Add the exact variance equation, specify whether Ax/BoTorch receives variance or standard error, and state the estimand:

> “The surrogate targets the expected response of a newly printed article at design $x$. Round-1 fixed-noise variances were [equations]. Beginning with round 3, $\sigma_{t}^2$ is lower-bounded by $(0.0072\bar t)^2$ and the analogous rebound-score variance is estimated from replicate articles rather than repeated drops alone.”

Do not propagate mass uncertainty into a quantity still labeled physical energy until Probe 5 is resolved.

### Probe 11: Is SAASBO justified, or merely asserted?

**Hostile referee:** Why use a sparse high-dimensional prior for five design variables and seven mapped observations? Where is the evidence that it improves calibration over an ordinary Gaussian process?

**Author’s best answer from the materials:** Section 3.4 gives a plausible rationale: marginalizing hyperparameters may improve uncertainty quantification at very small sample sizes (lines 932–943). It also promises leave-one-out checks and comparison against a standard single-task Matérn-5/2 GP.

The current evidence does not establish superiority. Results state that the sparsity prior has not identified a dominant subspace and that no input separates from the equal-share line (lines 1190–1207). The SAASBO-versus-standard-GP calibration audit is explicitly pending (lines 1229–1239). SAASBO is therefore an implemented modeling choice, not a demonstrated methodological advantage.

There is an additional specification ambiguity. Section 3.4 describes a surrogate over the five design variables (lines 905–923), but the feature-importance result uses “the five design variables plus printed mass” (lines 1190–1194). If printed mass is a sixth input to the objective GPs, its unobserved value for a candidate must be predicted and uncertainty propagated. The Methods instead describe mass as a separately learned tracking metric (lines 954–969). The recommendation CSV gives every trial the same predicted mass, 20.22 g, which does not resolve that dependency.

**Verdict: WOUNDED.** SAASBO is defensible as a preregistered model, but its superiority is asserted rather than shown, and the input vector is unclear.

**Concrete edits:**

- Replace lines 937–943 with:

> “SAASBO is the prespecified round-1 surrogate. At the current dimensionality and sample size we do not claim it outperforms a standard Matérn-5/2 GP; that comparison is pending the budget-matched audit.”

- State explicitly whether the objective input is $x=(R,H,\theta,d_s,d_t)$ or $(x,m_{\mathrm{printed}})$. If mass is included, give the joint or plug-in prediction procedure and uncertainty propagation. If not, remove printed mass from the feature-importance input list.

### Probe 12: Do Methods and Results agree numerically?

**Hostile referee:** Your own table lists values below one. How can the Results say seven of eight articles amplify the peak?

**Author’s best answer from the materials:** They cannot. The CSV contains five values above one:

- bpx68c: 1.0111;
- 9hhbkp: 1.0183;
- nvxsrv: 1.0275;
- autv5r: 1.0404;
- bag26v: 1.0616.

Three are below one:

- 6lhxfy: 0.8931;
- 6nheas: 0.9970;
- amdjwm: 0.9805.

Thus main text lines 1108–1111 and Discussion lines 1253–1255 are false as written. The absolute range is $1.06162-0.89308=0.16854$, or 0.169 ratio units. Calling that a “17% spread” is ambiguous; it is 16.9 percentage points in the ratio and 18.9% relative to the minimum.

The mass correlation is correct for the seven mapped articles: Pearson $r=0.829$, $p=0.021$. The manuscript reports $r=0.83$ but should avoid implying causation because mass is structurally confounded with geometry. The rebound values in Table 1 agree with the stated linear formula.

A second reporting inconsistency appears between Section 3.3 and the table caption. Lines 805–807 say objectives are reported as stabilized means with per-drop standard errors, while Table 1 lines 1135–1140 says $t_{180}$ is mean ± one standard deviation. The listed values match the CSV `t180_sd`, so the table contains SD, not SEM.

**Verdict: FAILS.** The amplification count is a direct numerical error; the uncertainty label is internally inconsistent.

**Concrete edits:**

- Replace Results lines 1108–1112 with:

> “Measured $t_{180}$ spans 0.893 to 1.062. Five of eight articles have mean ratios above unity, two lie slightly below unity at 0.997 and 0.980, and one attenuates the peak by about 11% at 0.893. The absolute design range is 0.169 in $t_{180}$.”

## Limitations of this review

- Only aggregated article-level CSVs were supplied, not raw acceleration records or processing code. I could check equations and reported summaries but could not independently regenerate peaks, contact times, or filtering outputs.
- The attached bibliography supplies metadata for SAE J211/1 and Bernstein’s paper, not their full text. I therefore assessed standards terminology only from claims present in the manuscript and references file.
- The 0.72% article-level coefficient of variation comes from the manuscript’s stated five-print study; those five article records were not among the supplied CSVs, so I checked disclosure consistency rather than re-estimating it.

## Discretionary analytical decisions

- Treated $t_{180}>1$ literally as amplification and $t_{180}<1$ as attenuation; no practical-equivalence band was imposed because none is defined in the manuscript.
- Recomputed per-drop SEM as CSV `t180_sd` divided by $\sqrt{99}$ to match the stated 101 drops minus two warm-ups.
- Used the exact gravitational acceleration $g=9.80665\ \mathrm{m/s^2}$ and $h=1.524$ m when checking the rebound-energy table.
- Treated the seven rows with mapped geometry and nonmissing mass as the correlation sample.
- Classified omissions that prevent numerical regeneration as FAILS rather than WOUNDED, even where a versioned code release might eventually supply the missing details.
