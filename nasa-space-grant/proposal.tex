\section{Introduction and Motivation}
% =============================================================================

I am applying for a Utah NASA Space Grant Consortium fellowship to support a
year of focused research on \textbf{multi-material 3D-printed
tensegrity-inspired structures for spacecraft and payload energy absorption}.
Every NASA mission that lands on a planet, returns a sample to Earth, or
deploys a CubeSat from a launch vehicle has to dissipate a large pulse of
kinetic energy in a very short time.  Mars Pathfinder and the Mars Exploration
Rovers landed on inflatable airbags qualified through 52 vacuum-chamber drop
tests at velocities up to $\sim$25~m/s \citep{adams2004merairbag}; later
crashworthy planetary landers have used composite-honeycomb energy absorbers
designed to a $\sim$20~psi crush stress and a 20~g floor-acceleration limit
\citep{jackson2014honeycomb}; CubeSats and instruments routinely sit on foam
or elastomer isolators to survive launch shock and vibration
\citep{nasagsfc2018gevs}.  These incumbent attenuators work, but they are
heavy, single-use, and tuned to a narrow load profile---a design space first
formalized by \citet{cloutier1966landing} as a trade among crushing stress,
density, and stroke.

\textbf{Tensegrity-inspired structures}---networks of rigid struts suspended
in a continuous web of tension elements---offer a programmable alternative.
NASA Ames' NASA Innovative Advanced Concepts (NIAC)-funded \emph{Super Ball Bot} project demonstrated that
tensegrity geometries can survive impacts on the order of 15~m/s and combine
landing, payload protection, and surface mobility in a single packable
structure \citep{caluwaerts2014superball,agogino2018superball,sabelhaus2015system,vespignani2018design},
and the concept has been extended to ocean-world rideshare rovers
\citep{deitrich2022titan}.  What has been missing is a fast, low-cost way to
design and \emph{experimentally} validate tensegrity-inspired geometries
against NASA-relevant impact scenarios.  Recent work shows that
tensegrity-inspired unit cells can be directly 3D-printed on consumer-grade
fused deposition modeling (FDM) hardware and retain a load-limiting plateau under drop-weight impact
\citep{pajunen2019design}.  Multi-material rigid--soft FDM (rigid struts +
flexible tension skins) yields tunable energy absorption without delamination
\citep{ye2023multimaterial,khatri2024energy}, and my lab has access to a
dual-nozzle \textbf{Bambu Lab H2D} printer, which makes this multi-material
workflow practical for a single-student campaign.

I propose to use that capability to run a focused, year-long
\textbf{print--test--optimize} campaign aimed squarely at two NASA-relevant
load cases: (1) low-velocity \emph{lander-class} crush attenuation and
(2) launch-/deployment-class \emph{shock isolation} for small-payload
(CubeSat-class) hardware.  This work directly supports the Utah NASA Space
Grant Consortium's mission of preparing students for the NASA workforce and
aligns with two Space Technology Mission Directorate (STMD) priorities visible across the Ames NIAC tensegrity line
and the In-Space Manufacturing (ISM) / FabLab roadmap: lightweight
load-limiting structures and multi-material additive manufacturing (AM)
\citep{nasa2022strategic,prater2019summary,agogino2018superball}.

\section{Background and Relation to NASA Priorities}
% =============================================================================

\textbf{Tensegrity in space systems.} Tensegrity structures have a long
history in NASA-funded research \citep{skelton2009tensegrity,sultan2009tensegrity}.
Caluwaerts, Agogino, SunSpiral, and collaborators at
NASA Ames proposed tensegrity-based planetary probes that combine landing and
surface mobility in a single deployable structure
\citep{caluwaerts2014superball,agogino2018superball,sabelhaus2015system,vespignani2018design},
and the line continues into Titan-class ocean-world rideshare concepts
\citep{deitrich2022titan}.  Subsequent work has shown that 3D-printable
\emph{tensegrity-inspired} unit cells exhibit a long
plateau in their force--displacement response under drop-weight impact---exactly
the behavior desired in a crush core, where peak transmitted force must stay
below a hardware/biological tolerance \citep{pajunen2019design}.  Compared
to honeycomb or foam, tensegrity
geometries can be \emph{tuned} (struts, cables, topology) without changing
manufacturing process, opening the door to mission-specific designs.

\textbf{Multi-material 3D printing as an enabling technology.}  In-space
manufacturing is an active NASA priority: the ISS has hosted FDM printers
since 2014, and on-demand fabrication is expected to be central to long-duration
missions \citep{prater2019summary}.  Demonstrating that tensegrity
energy-absorbers can be reliably produced on a single FDM platform with two
materials (PLA struts + TPU tension elements) contributes to that
broader trajectory while staying achievable within a single fellowship year.
The \textbf{Bambu Lab H2D}'s dual-nozzle architecture and Automatic Material
System let me switch between rigid PLA and flexible TPU within a single
print, dramatically reducing iteration time on each new geometry.  I expect
to print on the order of \textbf{50--100 specimens} over the year.

\textbf{Bayesian optimization of impact-absorbers.}  The design space (strut
diameter and length, TPU skin thickness, tiling and topology) is too large to
sweep exhaustively, and physical specimens are expensive in time, not just
filament.  Bayesian optimization (BO) \citep{shahriari2016taking,frazier2018tutorial,zhang2021bo}
fits the problem: each printed specimen updates a Gaussian process surrogate
that recommends the next geometry to print and test.  BO has been
applied to architected and metamaterial design \citep{mo2023accelerated,zhang2021bo}
and to closed-loop materials discovery in domains ranging from concrete
\citep{ament2023sustainable} to organic emitters
\citep{striethkalthoff2024delocalized}; here I apply it directly to a
NASA-relevant impact-attenuation problem with fully physical drop-weight tests,
which the BO-in-AM literature identifies as an open gap \citep{zhang2021bo}.

\section{Research Objectives}
% =============================================================================

I have three concrete, measurable objectives for the fellowship year:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.2em]
    \item \textbf{Build a parameterized tensegrity-inspired unit-cell family}
    that prints reliably on the Bambu Lab H2D in PLA + TPU, with verified
    geometric fidelity (computed tomography (CT) or caliper-and-mass) on at least 5 baseline
    geometries.
    \item \textbf{Quantify NASA-relevant impact response} for $\geq 30$
    distinct geometries via quasi-static compression and drop-weight tests,
    extracting peak transmitted force, specific energy absorption (SEA),
    and crush-plateau efficiency.  Compare against a 3D-printed honeycomb
    baseline of equal areal density.
    \item \textbf{Close the loop with Bayesian optimization} to find geometries
    that minimize peak transmitted force at a target SEA for two
    NASA-motivated load cases: a low-velocity ``lander'' drop ($\sim$3--6~m/s,
    20--50~J) and a high-frequency ``CubeSat'' shock pulse
    \citep{nasagsfc2018gevs}.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Proposed Approach and Methods}
% =============================================================================

\textbf{Design.} I will parameterize a family of tensegrity-inspired unit
cells in Fusion~360---strut diameter and length, TPU skin thickness,
connectivity topology, and tiling pattern---following the wrapping-based
core/skin strategy in \citep{ye2023multimaterial} so that PLA struts
remain mechanically interlocked with continuous TPU tension members and do
not delaminate under cyclic loading \citep{khatri2024energy}.

\textbf{Fabrication.} All specimens will be printed on the
\textbf{Bambu Lab H2D} in the Baird lab, using its dual-nozzle, multi-material
capability to produce PLA + TPU parts in a single build.  I will keep a
\textbf{print log} (slicer settings, AMS routing, post-print mass and
geometric checks) so that downstream test data can be traced back to a
specific build configuration.  Early in the year I will validate
PLA--TPU interface robustness on a reduced set of test coupons before
committing to full unit-cell prints.

\textbf{Testing.} Two complementary protocols, both NASA-relevant:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em,itemsep=0.1em]
    \item \emph{Quasi-static compression} on a benchtop universal testing
    machine to extract force--displacement curves, plateau stress, and
    densification strain (analogous to the characterization done for
    honeycomb crush cores in entry, descent, and landing (EDL) design).
    \item \emph{Drop-weight impact} from instrumented fixed-mass drops to
    measure peak transmitted force and SEA at lander-relevant velocities,
    following the protocol in \citep{pajunen2019design}.
    A piezoelectric accelerometer or load cell at the base captures the
    transmitted pulse.
\end{itemize}
A 3D-printed aluminum-equivalent honeycomb baseline of equal areal density
will be tested under the same conditions to anchor performance claims to a
NASA incumbent.

\textbf{Bayesian optimization loop.} I will implement a BO loop in Python
using BoTorch / Ax \citep{balandat2020botorch} that operates directly on
experimental measurements.  Each round of physical tests updates a Gaussian
process surrogate; the acquisition function (expected hypervolume improvement
for the multi-objective peak-force / SEA trade-off) recommends the next batch
of $\sim$3--5 designs to print and test.  This closed loop is the experimental
analogue of the recent multifidelity BO framework for architected
materials \citep{mo2023accelerated}, but here grounded entirely in physical
data so that conclusions are not
limited by simulation fidelity---an important property for NASA-relevant
impact problems where rate-dependent TPU damping is hard to model accurately.

\section{Expected Outcomes and Broader Impact}
% =============================================================================

By the end of the fellowship year I expect to deliver:
(i) a publicly released data set of $\sim$50--100 PLA/TPU tensegrity-inspired
specimens with quasi-static and drop-weight impact characterization,
(ii) an open-source Python implementation of the experimental BO loop, and
(iii) a poster or talk at the annual UNSGC conference and a draft journal
manuscript targeting
\emph{Smart Materials and Structures} or the \emph{ASME Journal of Mechanical
Design}.

The broader impact is twofold.  Scientifically, this is an early empirical
demonstration that a single student, with one multi-material FDM printer
and a Bayesian optimization loop, can systematically discover impact
attenuators that out-perform a NASA-incumbent geometry on a relevant load
case.  The resulting dataset and methodology are designed to seed follow-on
proposals to NASA NIAC (in the Super Ball Bot lineage
\citep{agogino2018superball,deitrich2022titan}), STMD/SBIR ISM calls, and
NASA EPSCoR, with a clear materials-qualification pathway from PLA/TPU
discovery specimens toward space-qualified polymers (PEI/PEKK/PEEK) screened
under MISSE-style protocols \citep{finckenor2023misse}.  Educationally, this
fellowship would give me hands-on training in exactly the skills NASA's
strategic plan calls out for the future workforce \citep{nasa2022strategic}:
multi-material additive manufacturing, mechanical testing, and data-driven
design.  I plan to use the experience as a springboard into graduate study
in aerospace structures or in-space manufacturing.
