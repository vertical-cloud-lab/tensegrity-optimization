# 1. REVIEW A — Reviewer A (Cordero-Style Persona: Architected Materials and Energy-Absorption Mechanics)

## Summary of Contribution as Understood

The authors propose a non-prismatic, hexagon-based, tensegrity-inspired lattice configuration fabricated via FDM from TPU and PLA. The stated goal is to protect a payload under high-impact conditions, with the motivating application being future Mars landers. Bayesian optimization is employed to tune the unit-cell geometry for orientation-insensitive impact absorption. Validation is pursued through controlled drop testing and field demonstrations across multiple impact angles.

## Strengths

**S1. Timely combination of tensegrity-inspired architecture with optimization.** The tensegrity-inspired lattice concept for impact absorption is grounded in a growing literature. Tensegrity structures are inherently free-standing, packable, and capable of absorbing large impact loads while maintaining load-bearing capability even after severe deformation (zhang2021optimizationforenergy pages 1-2, garanger2021softtensegritysystems pages 1-4). Combining this with Bayesian optimization for geometry is a contemporary and well-motivated approach.

**S2. Experimental orientation is commendable.** The decision to physically build and drop-test prototypes, rather than relying solely on simulation, is valuable. Drop testing of tensegrity-inspired structures has been shown to reveal important phenomena—such as material hysteresis-dominated energy dissipation and load-limiting behavior—that cannot be captured by quasi-static analysis alone (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 7-8, pajunen2019designandimpact pages 4-5).

**S3. Multi-angle testing to address anisotropy is well-motivated.** Orientation-dependent energy absorption is a recognized challenge in lattice structures, where functionally graded and hybrid gradient designs have been developed specifically to enhance directional robustness (wu2026energyabsorptionand pages 1-2). Acknowledging and testing for this is appropriate.

**S4. Use of Bayesian optimization for a physically expensive design loop.** Bayesian optimization is well-suited to problems where each evaluation is costly (here, physical drop tests). The literature demonstrates that BO can converge with as few as 15–250 high-fidelity evaluations for architected-material problems with 16–17 design variables (vangelatos2021strengththroughdefects pages 3-4, mo2023accelerateddesignof pages 6-7).

## Weaknesses

**W1. Complete absence of quantitative energy-absorption metrics.** The abstract reports no specific energy absorption (SEA), plateau stress, densification strain, stroke efficiency, crush force efficiency, or peak transmitted force values. In the established energy-absorption literature, an ideal absorber is assessed on at least four criteria: (a) the relationship between SEA and maximum transmitted stress, (b) plateau stress undulation, (c) densification efficiency, and (d) design tunability (shinde2022towardsanideal pages 1-2, shinde2022towardsanideal pages 4-5). Without any of these, the claim of "successful payload protection" is unverifiable. Even qualitative trends (e.g., SEA vs. relative density) would substantially strengthen the abstract.

**W2. No baseline comparison against established architectures.** The energy-absorption field has well-characterized baselines. For example, TPMS structures (diamond, gyroid) have been shown to outperform honeycomb and beam-lattice structures in SEA-to-maximum-stress ratios and densification efficiencies, while honeycomb structures occupy an intermediate position (shinde2022towardsanideal pages 1-2, shinde2022towardsanideal pages 13-18). TPU honeycombs fabricated by FDM achieve energy absorption up to 0.34 J/cm³ (bates20163dprintedpolyurethane pages 1-4), and bio-inspired curved-elliptical lattices achieve SEA of 24.6 J/g with crush force efficiency exceeding octet and BCC lattices by 15–35% (tuninetti2025biomimeticlatticestructures pages 12-14). Without benchmarking against any of these, the hexagon-based tensegrity cell's value proposition cannot be assessed.

**W3. Mechanistic rationale for the hexagon-based tensegrity unit cell is missing.** Why hexagonal? Why tensegrity-inspired rather than a true tensegrity (D-bar, truncated octahedron), which has analytical support for mass-efficient energy absorption that can exceed bent-buckled-beam approaches (goyal2019analyticalstudyof pages 1-2)? The term "tensegrity-like" is imprecise—the distinction between a true tensegrity (separate tension/compression elements in self-stress) and a monolithic FDM lattice that merely mimics the geometry is mechanistically important. Pajunen et al. (2019) carefully distinguish "tensegrity-inspired" structures fabricated as monolithic prints, acknowledging that these exhibit materially different energy-dissipation mechanisms (material hysteresis vs. elastic energy storage) from classical tensegrity (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 7-8).

**W4. Quasi-static vs. dynamic testing validity is unaddressed.** Polymers exhibit strain-rate-dependent mechanical properties, making strain rate testing critical for obtaining applicable data for impact scenarios (cronau2025energyabsorptionof pages 1-2). Cronau & Engstler (2025) demonstrate that quasi-static tests cannot represent material behavior under high strain rates and use drop-tower testing at 5 m/s to generate strain rates of ~150 s⁻¹ (cronau2025energyabsorptionof pages 2-4). The abstract mentions "controlled drop testing" and "field demonstrations" but provides no information on impact velocities, strain rates, or how these relate to the target application's loading regime.

**W5. Bayesian optimization loop is under-specified.** A well-posed BO for energy-absorbing architected materials requires: (a) clearly defined objective function(s) (e.g., maximize SEA subject to peak-force constraints), (b) enumerated design variables (e.g., strut widths, angles, relative density), (c) a strategy for handling experimental noise (drop-test variability), and (d) a surrogate model choice. In Mo et al. (2023), the BO explicitly optimizes energy absorption with constraints on plateau stress variation and relative density using a multifidelity Gaussian process, converging in ~15 high-fidelity experiments (mo2023accelerateddesignof pages 4-5, mo2023accelerateddesignof pages 6-7). Vangelatos et al. (2021) optimize 17 design variables with careful noise handling (7% SD) and an acquisition function strategy (vangelatos2021strengththroughdefects pages 4-5). The abstract provides none of these details—stating only "Bayesian optimization of lattice base-unit geometry" without specifying objectives, variables, or noise treatment.

**W6. FDM process-parameter and material variability are ignored.** FDM-printed parts exhibit inherent anisotropy, interlayer delamination, and void-induced crack initiation that can prematurely truncate energy absorption (bustihan2026recentadvancesin pages 4-6, bustihan2026recentadvancesin pages 2-4). Bates et al. (2016) show that TPU energy absorption is significantly dependent on both strain rate and cell orientation (bates20163dprintedpolyurethane pages 18-22, bates20163dprintedpolyurethane pages 1-4). The abstract does not acknowledge or control for these well-documented sources of variability.

**W7. "Successful payload protection" is not a scientific metric.** The claim of "successful payload protection across a range of testing conditions" is qualitative and unfalsifiable without defining success criteria (e.g., peak transmitted acceleration below X g, energy absorbed per unit mass above Y J/g). Mennu et al. (2024) demonstrate that even for passive energy-absorbing landing systems, peak acceleration is the critical metric, with pitch angle and impact speed identified as primary factors (mennu2024analysisofa pages 1-13, mennu2024analysisofa pages 13-14).

## Three Toughest Q&A Questions

**Q1.** "You claim 'successful payload protection'—can you provide the specific energy absorption (J/g), the peak transmitted force or acceleration (g), and the densification strain of your structure? How do these compare to a simple FDM-printed TPU hexagonal honeycomb of the same relative density, which Bates et al. (2016) characterized at 0.01–0.34 J/cm³?"

**Q2.** "Your Bayesian optimization has an objective—what is it, exactly? Is it minimizing peak transmitted force, maximizing SEA, minimizing the coefficient of variation of absorbed energy across orientations, or some multi-objective combination? How many evaluations has your BO loop completed, and how do you handle the stochastic noise inherent in drop-test measurements?"

**Q3.** "What is the mechanistic energy-absorption mode of your structure—is it elastic buckling and recovery (as in Pajunen et al.'s tensegrity-inspired prints), plastic hinge formation, material hysteresis, or some combination? Does your structure survive multiple impacts, and does it exhibit the load-limiting plateau behavior that makes tensegrity structures attractive for impact mitigation?"

## Numerical Recommendation

**Score: 2 / 5 — Major Revision Required**

*Justification:* The concept has merit as an early-stage exploration, but the abstract falls well below the quantitative and mechanistic rigor expected for an energy-absorption contribution. The absence of any crashworthiness metrics, baseline comparisons, or specification of the BO formulation means the abstract cannot currently demonstrate a contribution beyond what is already established in the tensegrity-for-impact and FDM-lattice-energy-absorption literatures. The work would be strengthened substantially by: reporting at least SEA, peak force, and densification strain; comparing against a simple honeycomb or foam baseline at matched relative density; and specifying the BO objective, design variables, and noise model.

# 2. REVIEW B — Reviewer B (Hofmann-Style Persona: Spacecraft Materials, EDL Heritage, Technology Infusion)

## Summary of Contribution as Understood

The authors present FDM-fabricated TPU/PLA tensegrity-inspired lattice structures as an impact-absorbing system for protecting payloads on future Mars landers. Bayesian optimization is employed to improve orientation-invariant energy absorption. Validation includes drop testing and "real-world field demonstrations."

## Strengths

**S1. Physical prototyping and testing is the right instinct.** The decision to build hardware and perform drop tests—including from multiple angles—reflects the kind of "build early, test often" mentality that accelerates technology maturation. This is encouraging for a student/early-career project.

**S2. Connecting structural innovation to a high-consequence application.** Using the Mars lander framing, while problematic (see weaknesses), does demonstrate the authors' ambition and awareness that passive energy-absorbing structures are relevant to planetary EDL. Tensegrity concepts for planetary landers have legitimate heritage in the literature (zhang2021optimizationforenergy pages 1-2, goyal2019analyticalstudyof pages 1-2, garanger2021softtensegritysystems pages 1-4).

**S3. Orientation-independence is a real design challenge for landers.** Landing orientation uncertainty is a genuine concern in EDL design. Mennu et al. (2024) show that pitch angle is a primary factor affecting peak acceleration in passive energy-absorbing landing systems (mennu2024analysisofa pages 1-13). Pursuing orientation-robust designs addresses a real engineering need.

## Weaknesses

**W1. PLA and TPU are not flight materials—this is a fundamental credibility problem.** PLA has a glass transition temperature of approximately 55–60°C and is biodegradable, hygroscopic, and prone to UV degradation. TPU, while more mechanically robust, is not qualified for vacuum, radiation, or thermal-cycling environments. For aerospace additive manufacturing in extreme environments, high-performance polymers such as PEEK, PEI (Ultem), and similar materials are required (aliberti2026additivemanufacturingof pages 6-7). Mars surface temperatures range from approximately −120°C to +20°C, with significant diurnal thermal cycling, UV flux unfiltered by a thick atmosphere, and near-vacuum pressures during transit. Neither PLA nor TPU has heritage or credible survivability in these conditions. The abstract makes no acknowledgment of this gap.

**W2. The Mars-lander framing overreaches the work's actual TRL.** This work is, at best, a TRL 1–2 terrestrial analog demonstrating a design methodology with surrogate materials. Framing it as contributing to "future Mars exploration techniques" and "future Mars landers" without acknowledging the enormous gap between FDM polymer prototypes and flight-qualified EDL hardware is misleading. For context, from the author's perspective at JPL, taking a concept from polymer prototype to flight-qualified metallic hardware for a mission involves materials qualification, environmental testing (ASTM E595 outgassing, thermal vacuum cycling, radiation exposure), structural analysis, and multi-year integration campaigns. The abstract should honestly position itself on the TRL ladder.

**W3. No comparison to heritage EDL energy-absorption systems.** Mars has seen multiple landing paradigms: Mars Pathfinder and MER used Vectran multi-layer airbags tested at impact velocities of ~60 ft/s (~18 m/s) on simulated rocky terrain (fasanella2009multiterrainearthlanding pages 3-6); Viking, Phoenix, and InSight used crushable aluminum honeycomb legs; and MSL/Perseverance used the Sky Crane hovering descent. Each of these is documented with specific mass fractions, peak deceleration limits, and energy-absorption capacities. The abstract neither cites nor compares against any of these heritage systems. Mennu et al. (2024) provide a contemporary example of how passive energy-absorbing composite structures for planetary payloads should be analyzed, including parametric studies of impact speed, pitch angle, and honeycomb sizing to achieve a 62% reduction in peak acceleration (mennu2024analysisofa pages 1-13).

**W4. No mission-relevant mass-efficiency metric.** A mission concept review would demand J/g (or J/kg) at a specified peak-deceleration constraint—not qualitative "successful payload protection." For tensegrity landers, Goyal et al. (2019) demonstrated that D-bar tensegrity systems achieve superior mass-to-energy-storage ratios compared to bent-buckled-beam alternatives (goyal2019analyticalstudyof pages 1-2). Zhang et al. (2021) noted that a tensegrity probe concept achieved a 50% payload mass fraction on Titan, more than twice that of conventional devices (zhang2021optimizationforenergy pages 1-2). These are the kinds of quantitative arguments a mission designer needs. The abstract provides none.

**W5. Environmental survivability is completely unaddressed.** Even setting aside PLA/TPU specifically, any material proposed for a Mars lander must address: (a) vacuum outgassing per ASTM E595 (total mass loss <1%, CVCM <0.1%), (b) thermal cycling over hundreds or thousands of cycles spanning the Martian temperature range, (c) UV and ionizing radiation exposure during transit and on the surface, and (d) long-duration storage (Mars transit is 7–9 months). FDM-printed polymers are known to exhibit interlayer delamination and void-induced crack initiation that worsens under environmental exposure (bustihan2026recentadvancesin pages 4-6, bustihan2026recentadvancesin pages 2-4). None of these concerns are mentioned.

**W6. "Real-world field demonstrations" is vague and potentially misleading.** What constitutes a "real-world field demonstration" for a Mars lander concept? Dropping a PLA/TPU prototype from a height on Earth does not validate Mars-relevant performance. Mars has 0.38 g surface gravity, ~6 mbar CO₂ atmosphere, and very different terrain properties than Earth surfaces. The phrase "high fidelity materials" is used without definition—high fidelity relative to what baseline?

**W7. No pathway from FDM polymer to flight-relevant materials and processes.** A credible abstract would sketch the material-transition roadmap: e.g., from FDM TPU/PLA proof-of-concept → SLM metallic lattice (Ti-6Al-4V, AlSi10Mg) → environmental qualification → integration with flight EDL architecture. Hofmann and colleagues at JPL have demonstrated this kind of technology infusion pathway for metallic glasses and compositionally graded metals, showing how AM prototypes translate to flight-qualified components through systematic material and process development (Hofmann et al., 2014; Oropeza et al., 2023). The abstract provides no such roadmap.

## Three Toughest Q&A Questions

**Q1.** "Have you evaluated whether PLA or TPU can survive the Mars transit environment—7+ months in vacuum with UV and radiation exposure—without catastrophic degradation, outgassing contamination of other spacecraft systems, or embrittlement at −120°C? What is your ASTM E595 data?"

**Q2.** "How does your structure's mass-specific energy absorption compare to the 13 kg/m² crushable aluminum honeycomb used on InSight's legs, or to the Vectran airbag system on MER that absorbed impacts at ~18 m/s? Can you provide a Ragone-style comparison (J/g vs. peak g-load) that would be meaningful to an EDL systems engineer?"

**Q3.** "If this is an early-stage methodology demonstrator—which is what the materials and test methods suggest—why is it framed as a Mars lander concept rather than as a Bayesian-optimization-driven design methodology for impact-absorbing cellular structures? Wouldn't reframing it as a generalizable design approach make the contribution clearer and more defensible?"

## Numerical Recommendation

**Score: 2 / 5 — Major Revision Required**

*Justification:* The Mars-lander framing undermines the abstract's credibility because the materials, processes, and testing conditions bear no relationship to the flight environment. However, if reframed as a terrestrial methodology demonstrator for Bayesian-optimized, orientation-robust impact absorbers—with a brief, honest acknowledgment that the approach could eventually be translated to flight-relevant materials—the work has value as an early-career innovation project. The current framing risks embarrassment at a symposium attended by practitioners who have actually put hardware on Mars.

# 3. SYNTHESIS

## Points of Agreement

Both reviewers converge on several critical assessments:

1. **The concept has early-stage merit** but is presented without the quantitative rigor expected even for a conference abstract. Both reviewers find the combination of tensegrity-inspired architecture, multi-angle testing, and Bayesian optimization to be a potentially interesting student-level contribution.

2. **The absence of quantitative metrics is the most damaging gap.** Neither reviewer can evaluate the claim of "successful payload protection" without SEA, peak transmitted force/acceleration, densification strain, or any other standard crashworthiness metric (shinde2022towardsanideal pages 1-2, shinde2022towardsanideal pages 4-5, mennu2024analysisofa pages 1-13).

3. **The lack of baseline comparisons renders the novelty claim unsupported.** Whether compared against established cellular-material topologies (Reviewer A's concern) or heritage EDL systems (Reviewer B's concern), the abstract fails to contextualize its contribution relative to the state of the art (shinde2022towardsanideal pages 1-2, zhang2021optimizationforenergy pages 1-2, fasanella2009multiterrainearthlanding pages 3-6).

4. **Both assign a score of 2/5 (Major Revision).** The work is not recommended for rejection outright because the concept and experimental initiative have value, but substantial revision is required before the abstract meets the standard for a TMS symposium on accelerating innovation.

## Conflicting Advice

1. **Framing:** Reviewer A would accept the Mars-lander framing if it were backed by rigorous energy-absorption mechanics and quantitative comparison to tensegrity and lattice baselines. Reviewer B fundamentally objects to the Mars-lander framing given the choice of PLA/TPU and the absence of any environmental survivability discussion. The resolution is likely Reviewer B's position: reframe as a terrestrial methodology demonstrator with a brief forward-looking statement about flight-material translation.

2. **Material focus:** Reviewer A sees PLA/TPU as acceptable for a proof-of-concept study of energy-absorption mechanics (following Bates et al. 2016 and Pajunen et al. 2019), provided the limitations are acknowledged. Reviewer B views PLA/TPU as disqualifying for any flight-relevant claim and would require at least a paragraph on the materials roadmap to metallic AM.

3. **Emphasis on testing rigor:** Reviewer A prioritizes strain-rate characterization and instrumented testing (accelerometers, force sensors, high-speed video per Pajunen et al. 2019 and Cronau & Engstler 2025). Reviewer B prioritizes environmental testing (outgassing, thermal cycling) and mission-relevant conditions (Mars gravity, terrain). Both are valid for their respective scopes.

## Prioritized List of Concrete Revisions

1. **Report quantitative metrics.** Include at minimum: SEA (J/g), peak transmitted acceleration (g), plateau stress (MPa), and densification strain (εD). Even preliminary values from the existing drop tests would transform the abstract.

2. **Reframe the application context honestly.** Replace "improve payload protection for future Mars landers" with language that positions this as a terrestrial analog or methodology demonstrator (TRL 1–2), with a forward-looking sentence about translation to flight-relevant materials.

3. **Add a baseline comparison.** Compare against at least one established architecture (e.g., FDM TPU hexagonal honeycomb at matched relative density) using the same test conditions. Bates et al. (2016) and Bustihan et al. (2025) provide readily available baselines (bustihan2025reusable3dprintedthermoplastic pages 1-2, bates20163dprintedpolyurethane pages 1-4).

4. **Specify the Bayesian optimization formulation.** State the objective function(s), design variable vector, constraint(s), surrogate model, acquisition function, and number of evaluations completed. The standards set by Mo et al. (2023) and Vangelatos et al. (2021) should guide this (mo2023accelerateddesignof pages 4-5, vangelatos2021strengththroughdefects pages 4-5).

5. **Clarify "tensegrity-like" vs. tensegrity.** Explain whether the structure has distinct tension and compression elements in self-stress (true tensegrity) or is a monolithic FDM print that mimics tensegrity geometry (tensegrity-inspired). Cite Pajunen et al. (2019) and Rimoli & Pal (2017) accordingly (pajunen2019designandimpact pages 7-8).

6. **Report test conditions and rate information.** Specify drop heights, impact velocities, strain rates, payload mass, and instrumentation. Acknowledge that polymers are strain-rate-sensitive and that quasi-static and dynamic behavior may differ substantially (cronau2025energyabsorptionof pages 1-2, cronau2025energyabsorptionof pages 2-4).

7. **Acknowledge material limitations for space environments.** Even briefly, note that PLA/TPU serve as surrogate materials and that flight-relevant implementation would require environmental qualification and transition to aerospace-grade polymers or metals (aliberti2026additivemanufacturingof pages 6-7).

8. **Define "successful payload protection."** Replace this qualitative phrase with a quantitative success criterion (e.g., "peak payload acceleration below 100 g" or "no structural failure at impact energies up to X J").
