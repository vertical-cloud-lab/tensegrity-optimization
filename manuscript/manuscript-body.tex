\begin{abstract}
Tensegrity-inspired structures of rigid and soft members could cushion
impacts, but their transmitted shock is hard to predict by simulation.
We report a drop-test-driven Bayesian optimization campaign over
three-strut prisms printed in one piece from polylactic acid (PLA) and
thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU). The search space has five geometry
variables, plus infill densities and filament settings from the third
batch. Each candidate is scaled to a common mass target before
printing, then 3D printed and drop-tested repeatedly. The optimizer
seeks low transmissibility (measured as a ratio of filtered top-to-base
peak accelerations) and low mass-weighted rebound (specimen mass times
the fraction of impact velocity returned at the top). Most specimens
amplify the peak shock. Over a quasi-random initial batch and three
model-recommended batches (35 specimens), the dominated hypervolume
grew 65 percent, and the model chose every design on the final Pareto
front. The final batch's best-predicted design lowered transmissibility
to 0.803, ten percent below the best initial-batch specimen. We printed
one batch three times to measure repeatability. Print-to-print scatter
in transmissibility was near one percent, and a seating check flagged
both large outliers, so single-print extremes stay provisional until
replicated. Code and data are available at \url{\repo}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Tensegrity structures are assemblies of rigid compression members
suspended within a continuous network of tension members. Their defining
trait is that no two rigid members contact one another, yielding lightweight assemblies with
high strength-to-weight ratios and tunable nonlinear mechanical
responses~\citep{skelton2009tensegrity, sultan2009tensegrity}. These
properties have made tensegrity attractive across scales, from
metamaterial unit cells~\citep{amendola2014experimental,
zhang2018tensegrity, fraternali2015tensegrity} to compliant impact-absorbing
robots developed for planetary landing~\citep{agogino2018superball,
caluwaerts2014superball, sabelhaus2015system, vespignani2018design,
deitrich2022titan}. Recent work has shown that \emph{tensegrity-inspired}
unit cells can be directly 3D-printed and exhibit load-limiting plateaus
under drop-weight impact~\citep{pajunen2019design}. Throughout, we use
\emph{tensegrity-inspired} for printable cells that borrow the separated
compression-member and tension-network geometry of tensegrity while
relaxing defining mechanical conditions of classical tensegrity. In the
present specimens, the printed soft tension elements are extensible,
the rigid struts may share printed junctions, and no pretension is activated or measured.
We therefore do not claim classical tensegrity, prestress-stabilized
behavior, or equivalence to an ideal cable-strut prism.

Multi-material fused-deposition modeling (FDM) can pair a rigid polymer
such as polylactic acid (PLA) with a soft, rate-dependent elastomer such as
thermoplastic polyurethane (TPU) on a single platform. It has been used to
fabricate rigid-soft architected energy absorbers, including thick-panel
origami metamaterials with PLA panels and TPU hinges~\citep{ye2023multimaterial}
and honeycombs of acrylonitrile butadiene styrene (ABS) and TPU with tunable
stiff/flexible proportions~\citep{khatri2024energy}. The same rigid-soft
co-printing capability makes it possible to fabricate diverse
tensegrity-inspired geometries, which adopt a
discrete-compression/continuous-tension topology rather than an origami or
honeycomb one. Although extruded TPU does not behave as an idealized
inextensible cable, its rate-dependent damping is a desirable property
for impact-absorption applications and complements the elastic stiffness
of the PLA compression members.

Designing such architected absorbers is a
\emph{design-of-experiments} problem. Strut geometry, tension-element
cross-section, connectivity, and unit-cell tiling span a large
configuration space. Each candidate must be physically printed
and tested, and the relevant impact-performance metrics (the shock
transmitted across the structure and the velocity it gives back to
the protected payload) are noisy and partially correlated. Bayesian
optimization (BO) suits this regime and has been applied to problems
ranging from hyperparameter tuning of
AlphaGo~\citep{chen2018bayesianoptimizationalphago} to sustainable
concrete~\citep{ament2023sustainable}, autonomous discovery of organic
laser emitters~\citep{striethkalthoff2024delocalized}, and architected
materials~\citep{mo2023accelerated, zhang2021bo}.
The gap this paper addresses is the combination of these two ideas.
Pajunen et~al.~\cite{pajunen2019design} printed tensegrity-inspired
cells under a single fabrication condition with no optimization loop,
and Mo et~al.~\cite{mo2023accelerated} closed a multifidelity BO loop
entirely in simulation. Among the studies reviewed here, prior work
separately demonstrates physical impact testing of printed
tensegrity-inspired structures, rigid-flexible additive fabrication,
and Bayesian optimization of architected materials. We found no prior
report combining these elements in a BO campaign over co-printed
multi-material tensegrity-inspired hardware with physical impact
measurements as the optimization ground truth. In the campaign reported
here, we close the recommend, fabricate, measure, and update cycle three
times. We trained the surrogate on a seed batch drawn from a Sobol
sequence, and each of three
successive model-recommended batches was fabricated, tested under the
identical protocol, and folded back into the refit that produced the
next.

The printed PLA/TPU $T_3$ prism studied here is a testbed for developing
and validating the closed-loop workflow, not flight hardware, and not the
end of the design space we intend to search. The planetary-lander setting
motivates the objectives (limit the shock delivered to a payload,
limit the velocity returned to it) and the constant-mass fairness
constraint. Our contribution is the loop itself, a stepping stone
toward optimizing tensegrity-inspired structures generally, independent
of the eventual application scale.

\paragraph{Contributions} This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item A parameterized family of multi-material 3D-printable
    tensegrity-inspired unit cells that uses TPU in place of the
    tension elements of the traditional tensegrity topology, with PLA
    members that carry compression most of the time. The cells are
    not prestressed, so neither member type acts as a pure tension or
    compression element. Under impact some tendons stretch while
    others buckle. The TPU tendons are anchored \emph{inside} the
    ends of each PLA strut, the strut acting as a rigid cage in which
    the tendons meeting at a given end join before exiting through
    discrete outlets. We report the resulting printable
    joint geometry. Pull-out strength and fatigue life remain
    untested and are an area of planned follow-on work. We compare
    candidate designs fairly through a constant-mass projection
    that scales every geometry to a common material budget before
    printing (Section~\ref{sec:methods:parameterization}).
  \item A characterized drop-tower protocol and two impact objectives
    for printed absorbers. We drop-test each specimen repeatedly
    (101 stabilized captures in the seed batch, about twenty recorded
    drops in later batches) with standardized
    SAE~J211 filtering~\citep{saej211}. The measured within-session
    variance justifies the smaller count in later batches
    (Section~\ref{sec:methods:testing}). The two per-specimen
    objectives, the transmissibility after channel-frequency-class (CFC)
    180 filtering and the mass-weighted rebound, are each characterized
    for repeatability before entering the optimizer.
  \item An experiment-driven BO loop, built on
    BoTorch/Ax~\citep{balandat2020botorch, pmlr-v293-olson25a} with a
    fully Bayesian sparse axis-aligned subspace surrogate
    (SAASBO)~\citep{eriksson2021saasbo}, that ingests the physical
    drop measurements, models per-print mass explicitly, and
    recommends each next batch of designs to fabricate. We report
    all four measured batches (35 specimens). The final
    Pareto front is entirely model-chosen, and the best-predicted
    design of the final batch had the lowest transmissibility
    measured in the campaign
    (Sections~\ref{sec:methods:bo} and~\ref{sec:results}).
  \item A measured error budget for drop-tower ranking of printed
    specimens. We printed and tested one full nine-design batch two
    more times (360 additional drops). Print-to-print and seating
    variation exceed the within-session scatter, a broadband seat gauge
    (Section~\ref{sec:methods:testing}) flags the largest single-print
    errors, and we adopted a between-seat
    replication rule in response (Section~\ref{sec:results:batches}).
\end{enumerate}

The completed evidence in this paper is restricted to single-cell,
fixed-topology $T_3$-prism-inspired specimens under one \emph{axial}
drop condition. Quasi-static testing is planned as an extension for
future work.
Off-axis and combined loading, controlled fatigue and
damage-accumulation testing, multi-cell behavior, and landing-attitude
variability are likewise future work (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
We limit the novelty and generality claims above to this restricted
prototype design space.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Tensegrity-Inspired Architectures for Energy Absorption}

Tensegrity prisms and their planar extensions exhibit a
softening-stiffening response under
compression~\citep{amendola2014experimental, zhang2018tensegrity,
fraternali2015tensegrity}, which is desirable for impact
mitigation because the structure can absorb a large fraction of the
input energy at a controlled force plateau.
Santos~\cite{santos2023towardanovel} characterized a tensegrity
energy-dissipation metamaterial with equivalent viscous damping
of $\sim$23\% and negligible residual strain per impact,
which suggests that tensegrity-inspired architectures suit
repeated-impact use.
The space program literature documents large-scale tensegrity-inspired
absorbers for planetary landing~\citep{agogino2018superball,
caluwaerts2014superball, sabelhaus2015system, vespignani2018design,
deitrich2022titan} and a long history of crushable energy-absorbing
landing systems~\citep{cloutier1966landing, adams2004merairbag,
jackson2014honeycomb}. Pajunen et~al.~\cite{pajunen2019design} directly
3D-printed truncated-tetrahedral tensegrity-inspired unit cells and
measured load-limiting plateaus, which motivates the present
focus on additively manufactured PLA/TPU composites.
Davami et~al.\ measured the dynamic energy absorption of additively
manufactured, shape-recovering lattices~\cite{davami2019dynamicenergyabsorption},
and Intrigila et~al.\ fabricated and characterized a bistable
tensegrity-like unit cell for lattice
metamaterials~\cite{intrigila2022fabricationandexperimental}, the closest
published analog to the present cells. Neither study used multi-material
FDM or closed-loop design optimization.
Table~\siref{tab:si-novelty} of the Supplementary Information
positions the present work against selected prior efforts spanning
the three elements combined here (impact-tested printed
tensegrity-inspired structures, rigid-flexible fabrication, and BO of
architected materials). None of the
studies we reviewed combines a multi-material FDM
tensegrity-inspired architecture with an \emph{experiment}-driven
optimization loop.



\subsection{Multi-Material 3D Printing of PLA/TPU Composites}

Multi-material rigid-soft FDM enables stiff/compliant combinations on a
single platform with tunable energy absorption, demonstrated for thick-panel
origami whose PLA panels are joined by TPU hinges~\cite{ye2023multimaterial}
and for ABS/TPU honeycombs~\cite{khatri2024energy}. Neither study is itself a
tensegrity, but both establish the rigid-soft co-printing route we adopt. Ye
et~al.~\cite{ye2023multimaterial}
introduced a \emph{wrapping-based} strategy in which rigid cores are
encapsulated by continuous soft skins, reported to reduce interface
delamination under cyclic loading, something we take inspiration from in our
designs.
Beyond these rigid-soft demonstrations, several studies show
that tensegrity-inspired architectures can be additively
manufactured with tunable mechanical response. Bauer
et~al.~\cite{bauer2021tensegrity} showed that 3D-printed tensegrity
metamaterials delocalize deformation to resist catastrophic failure, while
Pajunen et~al.~\cite{pajunen2021prestrain} tuned acoustic bandgaps in
3D-printed tensegrity-inspired lattices through prestrain, and
Sabouni-Zawadzka et~al.~\cite{sabounizawadzka2024experimental}
experimentally characterized 3D-printed tensegrity-inspired metamaterials
built on the simplex module. More directly relevant to impact, Almeida
et~al.~\cite{almeida2025highstrainrate} and Davami
et~al.~\cite{davami2025dynamicanalysis} characterized the high-strain-rate
and dynamic response of additively manufactured tensegrity-inspired
structures, and Wang et~al.~\cite{wang2026integrated} reported an
integrated fabrication-and-validation workflow for tensegrity-inspired
rigid-flexible metamaterials. None of these studies combines
multi-material FDM with an experiment-driven optimization loop.
Work on multi-material PLA/TPU sandwich and layered
composites~\citep{arifvianto2022mechanicalpropertiesof} reports
quantitative bounds on
stiffness, strength, and energy absorption for the same PLA/TPU pair
used here, while FDM PLA fatigue
data~\citep{ezeh2018onthefatigue, vanaei2021multiscaledamageanalysis}
can inform conservative design bounds for the rigid struts.
Architected multi-material lattices with co-printed compliant skins
have been designed for high specific energy
absorption~\citep{yavas2022designandfabrication}. Recent surveys review
additively manufactured polymeric energy
absorbers~\citep{bustihan2026recentadvancesin,
liu2026threedimensionalprintedlattice}. \todo{Optional: a short
paragraph on published TPU/PETG tensegrity dimension bounds. The Edison

\subsection{Bayesian Optimization for Architected-Material Design}

Bayesian optimization is a sequential, model-based strategy for
optimizing expensive black-box functions using a Gaussian-process
surrogate~\citep{shahriari2016taking, frazier2018tutorial}.%
\todo{@sgbaird to review the wording of this subsection and of the BO
  method (Section~\ref{sec:methods:bo}), including the objective naming
  and the SAASBO/qNEHVI entry of Table~\siref{tab:si-novelty} in the Supplementary Information (Madsen
  review part~2, item~5).}
Modern
implementations such as BoTorch~\citep{balandat2020botorch} and the Ax
platform built atop it~\citep{pmlr-v293-olson25a} support
parallel batched evaluations and many acquisition functions. Recent algorithmic advances include log expected improvement
(log-EI) for numerical robustness~\citep{ament2023logei}, noisy expected
hypervolume improvement (qNEHVI) for multi-objective
settings~\citep{daulton2021nehvi},
input-noise-robust acquisitions~\citep{daulton2022robust}, and
evolution-guided constrained multi-objective BO for self-driving
labs~\citep{low2024evolution}. Mixed/categorical search spaces, needed
for the planned connectivity-topology extension rather than the
continuous $T_3$-prism prototype studied here, have dedicated
treatments in Bayesian optimization of combinatorial structures
(BOCS)~\citep{baptista2018bocs} and the
one-hot-with-rounded-kernel construction
of~\citep{garridomerchan2020dealingwithcategorical}. BO has been
applied to architected-material
design~\citep{mo2023accelerated}
and to additive manufacturing~\citep{zhang2021bo}.
Mo et~al.~\cite{mo2023accelerated} in
particular established a multifidelity BO framework for architected
materials by fusing low-fidelity simulations with high-fidelity
ground truth via nonlinear information-fusion
priors~\citep{perdikaris2017nonlinear}. We shift the
emphasis from simulation surrogates to direct experimental data,
which suits materials whose constitutive response is difficult to
calibrate from first principles.

\subsection{Design Parameterization}
\label{sec:methods:parameterization}

We parameterize a family of tensegrity-inspired unit cells via four
groups of design variables. For the working $T_3$-prism prototype that
defines the initial BO search space (Section~\ref{sec:methods:bo}),
only the first two groups are active and all of their variables are
\emph{continuous}. We hold the connectivity-topology and tiling groups
fixed for this prototype and list them here as planned extensions of
the design family:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PLA compression members.} Strut diameter $d_s$ and
    length $\ell_s$, with the slenderness ratio $\ell_s / d_s$ bounded
    away from buckling-dominated regimes.
  \item \textbf{TPU tension elements.} Cross-sectional area $A_t$ (or
    equivalent diameter $d_t$ for circular cross-sections).
  \item \textbf{Connectivity topology (future extension).} Selected
    from a discrete set of candidate unit cells (e.g.,
    truncated-tetrahedral, prism-based, and octahedral variants). When
    activated, the design vector would encode this as a categorical
    choice. It is held fixed at the $T_3$ prism for the present
    campaign.
  \item \textbf{Unit-cell tiling (future extension).} Number of cells
    $N_x \times N_y \times N_z$ and the orientation of each cell
    relative to the impact direction. It is held fixed at a single
    cell here.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Working prototype and search-space evolution} We validated
the fabrication and test pipeline on the first instance of this family,
a three-strut, $T_3$-prism-inspired cell scaled to a
$\sim$50\,mm bounding box. In batches 1 and 2 the BO search space has
five continuous design variables over the ranges listed in
Table~\ref{tab:design-vars}. They are the base radius $R$, cell height $H$,
twist angle $\theta$, a single strut diameter $d_s$, and a single
cable (tendon) diameter $d_t$. We treat the cable diameter $d_t$ as a
\emph{continuous} variable on $[3.0, 5.5]$\,\si{mm}. From batch 3 the
space grows to include the print
process: two per-specimen sparse-infill densities (strut and tendon,
each 12 to 35\%) chosen by the acquisition, and four filament
settings (PLA and TPU nozzle temperature and maximum volumetric
speed). The filament settings are physically one value per print
job, so we draw them as one quasi-random point per batch, and they
remain confounded with batch until a round varies them within a
plate (Section~\ref{sec:methods:bo}). Under the prism's
$D_3$ symmetry all struts are equivalent, and we also give all cables
one diameter. We therefore use a single strut-diameter axis and a single
cable-diameter axis, leaving room to relax
to per-orbit (saddle, top, bottom) or fully per-member diameters once
the individual orbits have been characterized.\todo{Cite primary
sources on $D_3$-symmetric versus per-member diameters. The Edison
literature brief \texttt{5191cf4d} lists candidate papers to read and
cite.} We hold the joint diameter $d_j$ fixed at
\SI{7.0}{mm}. It sets the printed strut-end cage
that houses the internal cable anchors
(Section~\ref{sec:methods:fabrication}), so it is constrained primarily by the
TPU-tendon outlet count and the dual-nozzle clearance rather than by
energy-absorption performance. Fixing it keeps the anchor geometry
constant across specimens so that the optimized variables are not
confounded by joint-strength changes. Relaxing $d_j$ is deferred to a
follow-on joint-design study. Figure~\ref{fig:printed-prototypes}
shows the parametric CAD model alongside an as-printed prototype of this
working $T_3$ prism.

\paragraph{Constant-mass projection and printability screens} Because
cell mass varies severalfold across the raw design box, an
unconstrained search would partly reward designs for carrying more
material rather than for their architecture. We therefore project
every candidate onto a constant material budget before printing.
Each design is uniformly scaled (all dimensions except the twist
angle) until its mass target is met, converged to within
\SI{0.15}{g} on rendered geometry. The target evolved with the
campaign. Batches 1 and 2 held \emph{solid} CAD mass
$\rho_{\mathrm{PLA}} V_{\mathrm{PLA}} + \rho_{\mathrm{TPU}}
V_{\mathrm{TPU}}$ at $m^{\star} = \SI{30.95}{g}$, the solid mass of
the reference prism. That budget does not hold printed grams
constant, because PLA struts print at partial infill while thin TPU
cables print near solid. As-printed masses spanned
\SIrange{18.50}{22.04}{g} across the tested seed specimens, a
coefficient of variation (CV, the standard deviation divided by the
mean) of 5.5\%, and \SIrange{17.91}{23.47}{g} (CV 8.7\%) across
batch 2. For those batches printed mass is therefore only approximately
constant, and the surrogate carries each specimen's weighed mass
explicitly (Section~\ref{sec:methods:bo}). From batch 3 the projection
instead holds \emph{printed} mass at \SI{20.23}{g}, the mass of the
printed reference specimen. A calibrated model predicts each design's
printed mass from its geometry, and the design is scaled until that
prediction equals the target (Supplementary Information). The printed
batches came in slightly light. Their mean masses were 0.2 to
\SI{0.6}{g} (1 to 3\%) below the target, and the CV within a batch was
1.7\% for batch 3 and 2.3\% for batch 4.

We also checked each projected design against two printability
limits, a \SI{250}{cm^3} size limit on $\pi R^2 H$ and a \SI{3.0}{mm}
minimum on the printed TPU cable diameter. Both were recorded as
warnings, and neither was used to reject or change a design. Because
the projection scales the whole cell, nine specimens printed with
cables of 2.51 to \SI{2.78}{mm}, including all four batch-4 specimens
that reduced the peak shock, and all nine printed. The screens and
the defect record behind them are in the Supplementary Information
(Section~\siref{si:printability}).

\paragraph{As-built resolution of the continuous variables} Although we
treat $d_s$ and $d_t$ as continuous, the nozzle diameter, the slicer
line width, and support interaction discretize the as-built
cross-sections, so the effective search granularity is coarser than the
nominal continuous box. The realized member diameter changes in steps
set by the slicer line width, and these steps determine the smallest CAD
change that gives a distinguishable as-built member. We weigh every specimen on a
\SI{0.01}{g} scale, and mass is the only as-built quantity measured for
every specimen. We have not yet measured the dimensions of the printed
members, so the geometry attached to the surrogate is the commanded
post-projection geometry. Repeated prints bound the fabrication
scatter in mass. One seed design printed three times has a mass
standard deviation of \SI{0.457}{g}, the value used in the observation
noise model (Section~\ref{sec:methods:bo}), and the three prints of
the batch-3 plate differ by a per-print flow offset of up to
\SI{0.48}{g} rather than by random scatter
(Section~\siref{si:printability}).

\subsection{Experimental Characterization}
\label{sec:methods:testing}

Every specimen was drop-tested on one instrumented fixture under one
protocol. This section describes the fixture, the instrumentation and
filtering, the two objectives computed from each session, and the
replication checks that bound what a single session can show.

\paragraph{Drop-tower fixture} We characterized the specimens primarily
by repeated drop-weight impact on a Lansmont Model~23 shock test system.
The printed specimen rode upright on the free-fall carriage plate,
which was raised \SI{60}{in} (\SI{1.524}{m}) along guide rails and
released. The plate landed on a single \SI{0.5}{in} Shore 60~OO
polyurethane cushioning sheet, which shaped the landing into a single
sharp base pulse of roughly \SI{1.6}{ms} duration. We selected this
impact surface in a blinded crossover experiment over three candidate
mat arrangements. Unlike a felt stack, it did not measurably compact
over hundred-drop sessions (the seed sessions were specified at one
hundred drops). We monitored rig health continuously through the impact
velocity change $\Delta v$ recovered from the base accelerometer. Over
an uninterrupted 100-drop characterization session, the $\Delta v$
coefficient of variation was 0.54\% with no drift. We checked every
campaign session against the healthy tower baseline. Details,
including the within-session mat warm-up and recovery pattern, are in
the Supplementary Information. One drop took roughly
\SI{42}{s}, so a seed-batch characterization took about ninety minutes
per specimen and a later-batch session about fifteen.

\paragraph{Instrumentation and signal processing} A single-axis
piezoelectric accelerometer on the carriage plate recorded the base
input and triggered the acquisition. A triaxial accelerometer (Dytran
3133A4) seated in a printed pocket at the specimen's top vertex recorded
the transmitted response. We cross-calibrated the two base-capable
channels in place over fifteen co-located drops (ratio
$0.953 \pm 0.001$ at matched filtering) and archived the transducer
settings with the data. Signals were captured at
\SI{1.25}{MHz} over a \SI{100}{ms} record with a \SI{2}{ms}
pre-trigger. We low-pass filtered all channels with the SAE~J211/1
channel-frequency-class (CFC) filters~\citep{saej211}, implemented from
the standard's published coefficients with the two-pass corner
frequency handled explicitly. A common implementation shortcut narrows
the passband by roughly 20\%, which we identified and corrected before
the campaign. All reported numbers use the corrected filter. CFC-180
is the reporting class for peak metrics, with CFC-1000 retained as a
higher-bandwidth diagnostic.

\paragraph{Per-specimen objectives and session protocol} Session
length changed as our understanding of the measurement noise improved,
so the drop counts differ by batch. Seed
sessions recorded 103 drops per specimen and contribute 101 stabilized
captures each (103 for one specimen, autv5r) after we discard the first
two drops of each session as seating warm-up. A pre-campaign
variance study on the commissioning datasets put the statistical
minimum at five stabilized drops after two warm-ups. The seed
sessions confirmed that the stabilized mean is insensitive to session
length. Their within-session per-drop CVs were 0.17 to 0.48\%, with
flat post-warm-up trends. A replay of the seed sessions truncated to
their first twenty stabilized drops left the transmissibility ranking
of all nine tested seed specimens unchanged. The largest shift of a
session mean was $+0.008$ (bag26v). Gaps between specimens adjacent in
the ranking were 0.002 to 0.087, and at twenty drops the standard error
of each mean (0.0002 to 0.0011) was still below the smallest gap. The closest pair (bpx68c and ajhby6) kept its order
because truncation shifted both upward. We therefore
shortened later sessions to about twenty drops per specimen. The
archived record lists 21 to 22 valid captures per batch-2 specimen
and 20 recorded drops per specimen in batches 3 and 4, against 101
valid captures per seed specimen. As before, we discarded the two
warm-up drops of every session. Exact per-session counts are in the
archived per-drop records. Shortening the sessions cut tower time
per specimen from about ninety minutes to about fifteen while
retaining roughly four times the minimum stabilized-drop budget. A
shorter session enters the surrogate with a proportionally larger standard
error, which the model downweights. The three prints of batch 3
(Section~\ref{sec:results:batches}) later showed that seating and
print-to-print variation limit what one session can claim far more
than session length does, so we spent the freed tower time on
re-seats. Each
session yields two objectives, each reported as a stabilized mean
with the per-drop scatter quoted as one standard deviation, and the
standard error of the mean enters the surrogate's noise model
(Section~\ref{sec:methods:bo}):
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    t_{180} &\;=\; \frac{\max_t\,\lVert \mathbf{a}_{\mathrm{top}}(t)\rVert_{\mathrm{CFC180}}}
                        {\max_t\,\lvert a_{\mathrm{base}}(t)\rvert_{\mathrm{CFC180}}},\\
    e_{\mathrm{reb}} &\;=\; \frac{g\, t_{\mathrm{second}}}{2\,\Delta v},
    \qquad
    E_{\mathrm{reb}} \;=\; e_{\mathrm{reb}}\, m\, g\, h,
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:objectives}
\end{equation}
where $t_{180}$ is the CFC-180 filtered peak-acceleration ratio,
which we call the \emph{transmissibility} throughout. It is the peak magnitude
of the triaxial top-vertex response over the peak base input within the
impact window. We use the term in the shock-testing sense that is
standard for cushioning and packaging drop tests, a ratio of filtered
peak accelerations across the specimen under a single transient, not the
steady-state frequency-response function of vibration isolation. The two
peaks need not be simultaneous, and the filter class is part of the
definition. We cite SAE~J211/1 only for the impact-channel
filtering. Values above unity indicate the structure amplifies the
peak shock reaching its top vertex; values below unity indicate
attenuation. The second objective is the \emph{mass-weighted rebound}
$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$, and lower is better, as for the transmissibility.
It is built on the rebound fraction $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$, the speed at
which the top vertex hops after impact divided by the measured base
velocity change $\Delta v$, that is, the fraction of the impact
velocity returned at the top. We estimate the hop speed from the time
to the second contact, $t_{\mathrm{second}}$, assuming the hop is
ballistic~\citep{bernstein1977listening}. $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ multiplies
$e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ by the specimen's weighed mass $m$, the gravitational
acceleration $g$, and the drop height $h$ to give millijoules per drop,
and the optimizer minimized it in all four batches. The tables and
figures report $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$, since it is the quantity the
optimizer used. Weighting by mass keeps grams in the objective,
because equal rebound fractions on specimens of different mass are not
equivalent outcomes for a payload. Where we quote rebound as a
dimensionless descriptor, we use the complement of $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$,
the velocity loss index $\mathrm{VLI} = 1 - e_{\mathrm{reb}}$, the
fraction of the impact velocity that the structure does not give back.
Higher VLI is better, and across the 53 tested specimens it ranges
from 0.937 to 0.986. The mass weighting matters most where printed mass
varied. In batches 3 and 4, which held printed mass constant, ranking
the specimens by $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ and by $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ gives
nearly the same order (Spearman rank correlation $\rho = 0.995$ over 27
specimens). In batches 1 and 2 the two orders agree less well
($\rho = 0.855$ over 17). Two caveats apply. First, we have not yet
checked the hop timing against synchronized video, and $\Delta v$ is
the base plate's velocity change, which may differ from the relative
velocity at impact. The rebound fraction is therefore a timing-based
measure and should not be read as a coefficient of restitution.
Second, $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ is a ranking score that penalizes designs,
and grams, that send more motion back to the payload. It is not a
measured energy (see the next paragraph). Once the hop timing is
validated, the rebound fraction could become a constraint rather than
an objective (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\paragraph{Rebound fraction rather than its square} Returned kinetic
energy would scale with the \emph{square} of a velocity ratio, so an
energy-based score would use $m\,e_{\mathrm{reb}}^2$. We keep the first
power. The hop time measures a velocity ratio, and $e_{\mathrm{reb}}^2$
would be an energy ratio only under a ballistic reading of the second
contact that we do not claim. Because $g h$ is the same for every drop,
$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ and $m\,e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ rank all 35 tested designs
identically and give the same Pareto front. Squaring changes the
outcome at the margins. Of the 595 pairs of designs,
$m\,e_{\mathrm{reb}}^2$ reverses the order of 21, and 16 of those 21
pairs differ by less than 5\% in $m\,e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ (Spearman
$\rho = 0.990$ between the two design orderings). Squaring also removes
r2d2c2 from the design-mean front. Eight of the 21 reversals come from
averaging repeated prints of the batch-3 designs. The mean of the
squares exceeds the square of the mean, so $m\,e_{\mathrm{reb}}^2$
penalizes a design whose prints rebound inconsistently, and that
penalty tracks print scatter rather than design performance. The
comparison and its per-design and per-specimen tables are archived
with the manuscript data
(\texttt{scripts/\allowbreak analysis/\allowbreak pareto\_rebound\_index\_variants.py}).

\paragraph{Session diagnostics and replication} We recorded ringdown
descriptors per drop as print-health and prestress
diagnostics~\citep{ashwear2014natural}, but they are not campaign
objectives. They are the fitted natural frequency $f_n$ (260 to
\SI{470}{Hz} for every specimen but one batch-4 design that rang at
\SI{764}{Hz}) and, where the exponential fit passes an $r^2 \ge 0.85$
gate, a damping ratio $\zeta$.
The within-session per-drop CV of $t_{180}$ spans 0.06 to 2.0\%
across all 53 tested specimens (0.17 to 0.48\% in the seed
batch). These
values describe repeated drops of the same physical specimen in one
seating and do not estimate print-to-print or seating-to-seating
repeatability. Serial dependence among drops may also make
$s/\sqrt{n_{\mathrm{drops}}}$ optimistic. As an advisory screen, we
computed a seat gauge for every session, the ratio of its CFC-1000 to
its CFC-180 transmissibility. It read 1.00 to 1.07 on most
sessions, and we treat a reading of 1.15 or more as a re-seat
candidate. Two replication
measurements bound what a single session can claim. The seed
attenuator, re-measured across a day and an independent re-seat,
agreed to 0.13\%. Three prints of the batch-3 designs put the
standard deviation between specimens and seatings at 0.0087 in
$t_{180}$, once sessions that fail the seat gauge or carry a drift
flag are set aside (Section~\ref{sec:results:batches}). The two
largest deviations, 10 and 20\% above their design medians, both came
with a failed gauge. We therefore adopted a
between-seat replication rule: a single-seating value that would
drive a decision (a batch extreme, a claimed attenuator, a front
membership) requires confirmation on an independent re-seat before
we treat it as a design property.

\paragraph{Planned quasi-static extension and complementary data
streams} The drop test loads the specimens in their elastic regime.
The crush lasts 1 to \SI{3}{ms} and the specimen recovers, so
crush-energy metrics such as specific energy absorption (SEA) and
compaction efficiency cannot be recovered from these captures by any
reprocessing. We plan a quasi-static compression campaign on a
benchtop load frame to supply them as a complementary
characterization of selected designs, not as objectives of the
drop-tower campaign. The metric definitions, the protocol we will
follow, and its scope limits are in the Supplementary Information
(Section~\siref{si:quasistatic}). High-speed video (\SI{959}{fps},
three clips per specimen) documented the post-impact rebound and
recovery, but it cannot resolve the millisecond-scale impact pulse,
so the accelerometer channels alone produce the objectives.

\subsection{Experiment-Driven Bayesian Optimization Loop}
\label{sec:methods:bo}

The optimization loop maintains a Gaussian-process (GP) surrogate over
the design space defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methods:parameterization}. After each round of
physical experiments, we refit the surrogate and maximize an
acquisition function to recommend the next batch of designs to
fabricate. We pose the campaign as a two-objective minimization of
the transmissibility $t_{180}$
and the mass-weighted rebound $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:objectives}. Minimizing $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ favors a small
rebound fraction on a light specimen. Both objectives serve the same payload.
The transmissibility $t_{180}$ bounds the peak shock the payload feels
during the impact, and $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ penalizes the motion thrown
back into it afterward.
The seed data showed an apparent trade-off, driven substantially by
the single strongest attenuator and not statistically resolved at seven
mapped specimens (Spearman $\rho = -0.39$, $p = 0.38$;
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). After four batches, the measured front
is nondegenerate (its members span both axes), and the rebound
fraction remains a candidate to become a constraint once its ballistic
reading is validated. Any later batch would keep
$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ as its second objective, so that its results stay
comparable with the existing front.
We implement the loop in the Ax platform~\citep{pmlr-v293-olson25a} over
BoTorch~\citep{balandat2020botorch}, with noisy expected hypervolume
improvement (qNEHVI)~\citep{daulton2021nehvi} as the multi-objective
acquisition. The $T_3$-prism search space is fully continuous,
including the process variables added in batch 3
(Table~\ref{tab:design-vars}), so no categorical
encoding is required. Mixed-search-space
constructions~\citep{garridomerchan2020dealingwithcategorical} and the BOCS
combinatorial baseline~\citep{baptista2018bocs} are the route for
adding connectivity topology as a categorical variable in a later
campaign. The input-noise-robust acquisition of Daulton
et~al.~\cite{daulton2022robust} and the evolution-guided constrained
variant of Low et~al.~\cite{low2024evolution} are held in reserve as
contingencies.

\paragraph{Surrogate and noise model} The surrogate is the fully
Bayesian sparse axis-aligned subspace model
(SAASBO)~\citep{eriksson2021saasbo}. It uses a Mat\'ern-5/2 kernel with
automatic relevance determination whose length scales carry strong
sparsity-inducing priors and are marginalized by Markov-chain Monte
Carlo with the No-U-Turn Sampler (NUTS) rather than point-estimated.
Every refit in the
campaign, through the batch-4 ingestion, uses this model with qNEHVI
on top (Ax 0.4 for the early rounds, Ax~0.5.0 from the batch-3
refit). We retain SAASBO, even though the
dimensionality is modest, because marginalizing the hyperparameters
tends to improve uncertainty quantification at the very small sample
sizes of physical campaigns. We audit the fitted model each round
with cross-validation (leave-one-out, and leave-one-design-out once
replicates entered the record) and length-scale-based
sensitivity diagnostics
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). Inputs are normalized to the unit cube
and outputs standardized. We supply observation noise per
observation as a fixed variance: for $t_{180}$ the squared standard
error of the stabilized mean,
$\sigma_t^2 = s_{\mathrm{drop}}^2 / n_{\mathrm{drops}}$, and for
$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ the drop-scatter term plus the propagated
print-to-print mass scatter,
$\sigma_E^2 = \sigma_{E,\mathrm{drops}}^2 +
(\partial E_{\mathrm{reb}}/\partial m)^2 \sigma_m^2$ with
$\sigma_m = \SI{0.457}{g}$ measured from the one design printed in
triplicate. The estimand this noise model implies is the
response of the tested specimen in its seating, not of a freshly
printed specimen at the same design. The pre-registered review of the
objective choices (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}) argued that the decision-relevant
target is the latter. To account for that target, we use the measured
replication evidence of Section~\ref{sec:results:batches} (second
and third prints of one batch) together with the
between-seat replication rule, rather than an inflated
per-specimen noise term. We ingested the nine second-print specimens,
re-prints of the same designs, as distinct completed trials at identical design coordinates,
each with its own weighed mass and per-session standard error and with
no pre-pooling. Later refits therefore observe measured
design-level replicates directly. Because per-session variances are
supplied as known, replicate disagreement is not absorbed by a
separately inferred noise parameter. Instead, it widens the posterior
through the fit itself. The third print was tested after the last
refit reported here and has not yet entered the fit. SAASBO is the
prespecified surrogate. We make no claim that it outperforms
a standard single-task GP at this dimensionality and sample size,
and the planned head-to-head calibration comparison against a
point-estimated GP with the same kernel remains future work.

\paragraph{Mass treatment} Because the constant \emph{solid}-mass
projection of batches 1 and 2 does not hold printed grams constant
(Section~\ref{sec:methods:parameterization}), printed mass correlates
with the shape variables in the seed batch, and with $t_{180}$
($r = 0.83$, $n = 7$, dominated by the lightest specimen). We therefore
keep mass explicit in the loop. The second objective,
$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$, is in absolute millijoules computed with each
specimen's weighed mass, so added grams are penalized through the
motion they send back to the payload. The transmissibility $t_{180}$
stays a ratio on purpose, because payload peak acceleration is a
damage criterion that does not scale with specimen mass. The objective surrogates take
the design coordinates of Table~\ref{tab:design-vars} as their
inputs. We attach printed mass as a separately modeled
\emph{tracking metric}, so the model learns as-printed mass as a
function of the design coordinates and every recommendation reports a
predicted printed mass. In the diagnostic refit behind the
seed-state length-scale diagnostics (Supplementary Information), we
additionally include printed mass as an input to probe this confound. With the calibrated
printed-mass model (Supplementary Information), we then held
\emph{printed} grams constant in batches 3 and 4. Across all 53 tested
specimens, printed mass explains about 1\% of the variance in
transmissibility ($r = 0.12$), so the seed batch's mass confound was
removed by the experimental design rather than adjusted away in the
analysis (Section~\ref{sec:results:audits}).

\paragraph{Budget and seeding} We drew the nine seed designs as the
first points of a scrambled Sobol
sequence~\citep{sobol1967distribution, owen1998scrambling}, the
initialization step of the Ax platform, with a recorded seed (0). A
Sobol sequence is a quasi-random sequence that covers the
five-variable box more evenly than the same number of independent
random draws. We printed the nine designs together with the fixed
reference prism (S0). Optimization proceeds in
sequential batches of $q = 9$ prints, sized to one build-plate
generation cycle. Three model-recommended batches are reported here
(Ax trials 10 to 18, 28 to 36, and 37 to 45). The numbering gap
records a superseded draft batch that was regenerated, before
printing, when the batch-level filament handling was simplified.
From batch 3 the four filament settings take one quasi-random point
per batch, formally the settings of that batch's best-predicted
specimen, so those axes are confounded with batch and are reported as
unidentified rather than learned. We record all random seeds,
the full experiment state, and the recommendation tables
per round. Full computational reproducibility (the exact processing
pipeline and model settings) is carried by the campaign code. We plan to escalate to
trust-region BO (TuRBO)~\citep{eriksson2019turbo} only if
per-member-diameter extensions push the design space well beyond the
present regime. We retain a single-objective log-Expected-Improvement
run~\citep{ament2023logei} on $t_{180}$ as a baseline to
quantify the benefit of the multi-objective formulation. We will
archive the campaign code and recommended batches at a
Zenodo-minted DOI mirroring the GitHub repository.\todo{Insert the
Zenodo DOI and release version for the campaign code once the
repository snapshot is published.}

\subsection{Replicate Rank-Stability Study}
\label{sec:methods:rankstability}

Repeated prints of the batch-3 designs
(Section~\ref{sec:results:batches}) showed that a single specimen's
measurement can move between seatings and prints by more than the
design signal separating neighboring designs, which bounds any claim
that one design outranks another. We plan a replicate study to
quantify this bound. Among the campaign's in-sample designs, ranked by
the current posterior's predictions, we will select the best-predicted
design, the worst-predicted design, and one mid-pack
(``run-of-the-mill'') design, print a nominal three fresh specimens of
each, and drop-test every specimen under the standard protocol of
Section~\ref{sec:methods:testing}, including the between-seat
replication rule. The question is whether the predicted ordering
survives fabrication and seating variation. With three designs no
significance claim is available, so the primary readout is the
separation of the three repeat clusters relative to the within-design
(print-to-print plus seat-to-seat) scatter, reported alongside the
Spearman rank correlation between the predicted and measured
orderings. The reporting format and the statistical floor are given
in the Supplementary Information (Section~\siref{si:rankstability}).
We also plan a metal-analog comparison in which worst-, mid-, and
best-predicted designs are rebuilt from hollow aluminum rods and
stainless-steel threaded cables and ranked against their PLA/TPU
equivalents (Section~\siref{si:metal-validation}).

\subsection{Seed-Batch Measurements}
\label{sec:results:seed}

We printed all nine Sobol specimens and the reference prism and
declared them acceptable to test. Nine specimens (seven mapped Sobol
designs, the reference prism, and one specimen whose design mapping
is unconfirmed) completed seed characterization sessions, totaling
over 900 clean captures within a project-wide history of more than
4{,}000 instrumented drops. The two remaining seed designs (specs 03
and 06) were printed, but their drop sessions are absent from the
campaign record and they do not enter any fit or figure.
Table~\ref{tab:seed-results} reports the
stabilized objectives. Three findings frame the batch. First, the
measured $t_{180}$ spans 0.893 to 1.062. Six of nine tested specimens
\emph{amplify} the peak shock reaching the top vertex (means above
unity), and two sit marginally below unity (0.997 and 0.980). One
tested specimen (the print of Sobol spec~01, the thick-strut,
thin-cable, high-twist corner of the batch) had a mean $t_{180}$ of
0.893, indicating peak attenuation under the tested condition. This
specimen later reproduced its attenuation within 0.13\% across an
independent re-seat. It is the campaign's positive control for the
replication rule of Section~\ref{sec:methods:testing}.
Across the tested specimens, $t_{180}$ spans 0.169 (18.9\% of
the observed minimum). This between-specimen range far exceeds the
0.17 to
0.48\% within-session per-drop CV, but one mechanically tested
specimen per geometry cannot separate geometry effects
from print-to-print variation or other fabrication covariates.
Second, the objectives show an
apparent trade-off in this sample. The best attenuator has the
lowest velocity loss index of the batch (0.950) and the largest
$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ (\SI{13.9}{mJ} per drop), so the observed front is
nondegenerate. The trade-off rests largely on that single specimen,
though, and is not statistically resolved at this sample size
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). Third, among the eight mapped
specimens with weighed masses, printed mass and $t_{180}$ showed a
large sample
correlation ($r = 0.82$, $n = 8$). The association is confounded with
the design coordinates and is highly sensitive to the lightest
specimen ($r = 0.26$ when 6lhxfy is omitted), so it is not evidence of a
causal mass effect. It nonetheless motivated the mass-aware objective
formulation of Section~\ref{sec:methods:bo}, and the
constant-printed-mass batches later removed the confound
(Section~\ref{sec:results:audits}). Figure~\ref{fig:seed-designs} renders
the seed geometries to a common scale, showing how widely the
constant-mass box spans aspect ratio, member sizing, and twist.

\subsection{Model-Recommended Batches and Replication}
\label{sec:results:batches}

Table~\ref{tab:batch-results} reports the stabilized objectives for
the three model-recommended batches, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:front-evolution} overlays every measured specimen with
the non-dominated front after each batch. We evaluate Pareto
membership over specimens with both objectives and omit the unmapped
seed specimen amdjwm, whose $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ cannot be computed
without a weighed mass. Dominated hypervolume, evaluated
at the fixed reference point $(t_{180}, E_{\mathrm{reb}}) =
(1.35, \SI{15}{mJ})$, grew from 3.155 after the seed batch to 3.969
after batch 2 ($+25.8\%$), 4.007 after batch 3 ($+0.9\%$), and 5.195
after batch 4 ($+29.7\%$), a 65\% total gain over the seed front. We
have not run a budget-matched physical baseline of Sobol designs, so
the comparison the data support is against the campaign's own Sobol
seed. After batch 4, every member of the combined front (corny7,
corny6, drran5, drran3, r2d2c6) is a model-recommended design, and the
last seed specimen left the front when the batch-4 record dominated
it. These fronts score every specimen as measured, with each
repeated batch-3 design at its first print. Scoring each batch-3 design
instead at the mean of its screened prints
(Table~\siref{tab:si-round3-repeatability} of the Supplementary
Information) removes drran5 and drran3 from
the final front. The design-mean front is corny7, corny6, r2d2c2,
corny4, and r2d2c6, still entirely model-recommended, with r2d2c2's
membership provisional on its drift flag. Scored the same way, batch 3
added 0.1\% to the hypervolume instead of 0.9\%, so most of its apparent
gain came from favorable first prints.

\paragraph{Batch 2 (Ax trials 10 to 18)} We generated the first
recommended batch from the seed fit and printed it under the
constant-solid-mass projection, so its printed masses still spread
widely (17.91 to \SI{23.47}{g}). A regenerated constant-printed-mass
recommendation set is archived alongside the as-printed one, and every
result reported here is for the as-printed set. The batch grew the front almost entirely
on the $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ axis, and four batch-2 specimens joined the front.
Specimen r2d2c6 still holds the lowest $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ on record,
\SI{4.1}{mJ} per drop, and its velocity loss index of 0.986 ties two
later specimens (drran3 and corny4) for the highest. On the
transmissibility axis, the archived posterior predictions sat below
the measurements for nine of nine specimens, with a mean absolute
percentage error (MAPE) of 14.4\%. The worst case was r2d2c3
(predicted 0.910, measured 1.334), the strongest amplifier in the
campaign record. That nine-of-nine
optimism probably widened the refit posterior for every batch that followed.

\paragraph{Batch 3 (Ax trials 28 to 36)} Batch 3 was the first batch at
constant \emph{printed} mass (19.24 to \SI{20.06}{g}, CV 1.7\%) and
the first with the infill and filament axes active
(Section~\ref{sec:methods:parameterization}). Its first prints filled
the knee of the front without extending it ($+0.9\%$ hypervolume,
three specimens joined). Eight of nine specimens measured within 0.3
posterior standard deviations of their predictions (MAPE 3.8\%). Two
first-print results stood out. The best-predicted design (trial 32,
predicted 1.020) measured 1.251, then the strongest amplification on
record, and the batch's only attenuator (drran8, 0.980,
drift-flagged) was its only twisted design. Neither result held up on
the later prints described below.

\paragraph{Batch 4 (Ax trials 37 to 45)} The acquisition split the
batch between the wide, high-twist, thin-cable family it had been
steering toward since batch 2 and a previously unvisited tall,
low-twist corner. Four of nine specimens attenuate, all of them from
the wide family. Specimen corny7, the batch's best-predicted design (trial 37,
predicted $0.934 \pm 0.13$), measured $t_{180} = 0.8031 \pm 0.0025$,
the strongest attenuation in the program, 10\% below the best seed
specimen and 21\% below the reference prism. Its velocity loss index,
0.968 ($E_{\mathrm{reb}} = \SI{9.4}{mJ}$ per drop), is mid-pack, and it
dominates the seed attenuator on both
objectives (Fig.~\ref{fig:batch4-pred-vs-meas}). Within the
attenuating family, the archived predictions ranked the four
measurements perfectly (Spearman $\rho = +1.0$). Across all nine
specimens the rank correlation is $0.82$, although the five amplifier
predictions span only 0.9\% and so carry no ordering signal. The
batch MAPE was 6.0\%, and no specimen left its predictive band by more
than one standard deviation. The record itself beat its band by 1.0
standard deviation in the good direction. Per the between-seat replication rule, the batch-4
attenuation values, including the record, are single-seating
measurements whose re-seat confirmations are scheduled. One session
(corny5) carries a drift flag, so its mean is provisional. Another
(corny6) was erratic, with a per-drop CV of 2.0\%, but attenuates
under any reading of its record.

\paragraph{Replication: three prints of batch 3} We printed the batch-3
plate three times and drop-tested every specimen under the identical
protocol, 540 drops in all. In the later prints, specimens 2dran$N$ and
dran3$N$ re-print drran$N$ at the same Ax trial (round-3 print key).
Each of the nine designs therefore has three specimens
(Fig.~\ref{fig:three-print}; the per-print values are tabulated in
Table~\siref{tab:si-round3-repeatability} of the Supplementary
Information).
The three prints averaged 19.59, 19.86, and \SI{20.07}{g}. Neither
extreme of the first print recurred. Trial 32, whose first print
(drran7) measured 1.2513, then the strongest amplification on record,
measured 1.0286 and 1.0448 on its later prints, close to its
prediction of 1.020. The drift-flagged 0.9804 attenuator of trial 35
(drran8) measured 1.0109 and 0.9958. Trial 35 is the
lowest-transmissibility design in every print, but its screened mean
is 1.003, so batch 3 contains no reproducibly attenuating design.

We screen each session with the seat gauge of
Section~\ref{sec:methods:testing}, and every large same-design
deviation came with a failed gauge.
The four sessions that read 1.15 or more were each their design's
highest value, 4.5 to 19.8\% above the design's median over the three
prints. The 23 sessions below that threshold sat a median 0.2\% from
their design medians (at most 3.7\%, on a drift-flagged session), and
the gauge correlates with the deviation (Spearman $\rho = 0.52$,
$p = 0.006$). The third print supplied one new gross outlier, dran35
(trial 28), at 1.168 with a gauge of 2.43, against 1.037 and 1.060 for
its earlier prints. Its re-seat is pending, and the screened means
leave it out.

Setting aside the four gauge failures and the two drift-flagged
sessions leaves 21 of the 27. Fitting those 21 with one mean per
design plus one offset per print leaves a residual standard deviation
of 0.0087 in $t_{180}$ (10 degrees of freedom). That is below the
0.013 estimated from the first two prints alone, and still an order of
magnitude above the within-session standard errors (median 0.0006)
that the surrogate ingests. The second print's offset is $+0.008$ and
the third's $-0.003$, so the upward shift of the second print did not
recur. The third print was also the heaviest, so that shift was
probably not a mass effect. Over the screened sessions the intraclass correlation of
$t_{180}$ (the share of its variance that lies between designs) is
0.58, against 0.06 when every session is included. Most of the design
signal in batch 3 comes from trial 35. Without it, the other eight
design means span 1.028 to 1.045 (standard deviation 0.006), less than
the scatter of a single specimen. Single specimens therefore cannot rank
those eight, and the orderings from different prints agree poorly
(Spearman $\rho$ of 0.08, 0.67, and 0.20 between pairs of prints). The
frozen batch-3 predictions rank the nine design means with
$\rho = 0.75$ ($p = 0.02$) when each mean is adjusted for the print
offsets, and with $\rho = 0.55$ ($p = 0.13$) without that adjustment.
Against single prints they reach 0.20, 0.72, and 0.30.

$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ repeats far less well than transmissibility. For
each design the largest of its three values is 1.35 to 2.9 times the
smallest (5.2 against \SI{15.1}{mJ} at trial 30). The rank
correlations between prints are $-0.18$, 0.25, and 0.33, and the
scatter between prints (standard deviation \SI{3.4}{mJ}) exceeds the
spread of the design means (\SI{2.3}{mJ}).

The 1.2513 session had passed every within-session stability check
(per-drop CV 0.77\%, no drift), so a stable session is not enough to
show that its value reflects the design. We adopted the between-seat
replication rule of Section~\ref{sec:methods:testing} in response.
Across an independent re-seat, the seed attenuator agreed to within
0.13\%, so genuine attenuation can reproduce. The seat gauge
accompanies the rule as an advisory screen, and all four batch-4
attenuators pass the gauge (0.98 to 1.06). The nine second-print
specimens joined the surrogate's training set as distinct observations
at identical design coordinates (Section~\ref{sec:methods:bo}). None of
the third-print specimens enters the fits, the cross-validation, or
Fig.~\ref{fig:front-evolution}. Their sessions (2026-09-28 to
2026-10-02) postdate the most recent refit.

\subsection{Surrogate Audits Across the Campaign}
\label{sec:results:audits}

We audit each refit with leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV),
using one full NUTS refit per held-out specimen, and with length-scale
diagnostics. The seed-state SAASBO length-scale diagnostics
(Fig.~\siref{fig:si-sensitivity} of the Supplementary Information) show
no input separating decisively from an equal share of the model
sensitivity. At seven observations for six correlated inputs, these
are prior-sensitive model diagnostics only, and they support no claim
of variable relevance or ranking.

Held-out point accuracy \emph{declined} as the campaign grew. The
$t_{180}$ LOOCV MAPE was 2.9\% with rank correlation $+0.76$ on the
seed state (8 specimens, 6 model inputs), 5.4\% and $+0.60$ after
batch 2 (17 specimens), and 6.2\% and $+0.19$ after batch 3 (26
specimens, 12 inputs). The $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ MAPE was 29.3\%, 23.6\%, and
32.2\% at the same states. The fold-level records are archived
with the campaign data. Three changes coincided and are confounded:
the input space doubled from 6 to 12 while the data grew only from
17 to 26, four of the six added axes carry a single value per batch
and are therefore perfectly confounded with batch, and batch 3
itself added the campaign's largest outliers. The held-out posterior
shrinks predictions toward the pooled mean, so it misses the
campaign's extremes. We therefore read the predicted columns as a
record of what the model expected when it chose each design, rather
than as forecasts.
Batch 4's value was the design family it selected and the record it
found, not its point accuracy, and its best-predicted specimen was
the record.

Plain LOOCV also leaks once replicates exist. When one print of a
repeated design is held out, its twin remains in training at nearly
identical coordinates, so the fold tests interpolation rather than
prediction. We re-audited the full record with leave-one-design-out
folds (35 folds over 44 specimens, each drran/2dran pair held out
together, one full NUTS refit per fold). The $t_{180}$ MAPE is
5.0\% with rank correlation $+0.08$. While the measurements span
0.80 to 1.33, the held-out posterior stays near the pooled mean
(Fig.~\siref{fig:si-logocv} of the Supplementary Information). The
$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ MAPE is 39.4\% with rank correlation $-0.38$. Held-out
design-level $t_{180}$ skill is therefore near zero. Part of the
apparent $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ skill of the earlier states came from the
model fitting chance differences between prints, which matches the
poor print-to-print repeatability of $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ in
Fig.~\ref{fig:three-print}. The
comparison to the LOOCV trajectory is descriptive rather than a
controlled ablation. The states differ in specimen count, fold
structure, and the presence of the repeat data itself.

The correlation between printed mass and transmissibility fell from
$r = 0.82$ over the eight seed specimens to $0.12$ over all 53 tested
specimens ($r^2 = 0.01$), which suggests that the
constant-printed-mass projection removed the seed batch's main
confound. Within batch 4 alone the two
correlate strongly ($r = 0.85$). There, however, the wide, high-twist family
printed at 19.45 to \SI{19.64}{g} and the tall, low-twist family at
20.15 to \SI{20.58}{g}, so printed mass follows the design family. The
correlation within the batch therefore cannot be read as a mass
effect. We have not yet run the planned head-to-head calibration
comparison against a point-estimated single-task GP with the same
kernel, so we make no claim that SAASBO outperforms it here.



\paragraph{Studies pending} The following remain future work, and
none of their values appear in this section. The batch-4 attenuators
(the record specimen first) and the third-print outlier dran35 await
re-seats. The third print of the batch-3 designs has not yet entered
the surrogate fit or its cross-validation. A fifth batch was
generated, and regenerated once the second print entered the fit, but
not printed, and nothing from it appears here. The replicate
rank-stability study of Section~\ref{sec:methods:rankstability}
(best-, mid-, and worst-predicted in-sample designs re-printed in
nominal triplicate), the quasi-static crush campaign, and the
metal-analog comparison (Supplementary Information,
Section~\siref{si:metal-validation}) have not yet run.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\paragraph{What the campaign establishes} Across the four measured
batches, the end-to-end loop closed repeatedly: printed specimens,
instrumented characterizations, a fitted fully Bayesian surrogate,
and three successive model-recommended batches whose own
measurements fed the next refit. The measurement distinguishes the
tested specimens. The campaign $t_{180}$ means span 0.803 to 1.334,
far beyond within-session drop scatter. The three prints of batch 3
locate the resolution floor for a single specimen. Clean seatings of
different prints differ by a standard deviation of about 0.009, and a
broadband gauge flags occasional seating failures of 10 to 20\%. The
physics is less favorable than the cushioning intuition suggests.
At this scale and rate, most printed prisms \emph{amplify} the peak
shock reaching their top vertex (26 of 35 specimens measured above
unity). We therefore sharpened the campaign question from ``how much does
the architecture cushion'' to ``which corner of the design space
crosses into attenuation, and at what cost in rebound.'' The
batches answered that question, though not on the first
try. Batch 2's forecasts were optimistic on nine of nine specimens,
and batch 3's best-predicted design measured as its worst amplifier
on its first print, a seating artifact that its two later prints did
not repeat. Batch 4
then delivered four attenuators from the wide, high-twist,
thin-cable family, led by its best-predicted design at
$t_{180} = 0.803$. The strongest attenuators still tend to have
low velocity loss indices (0.938 and 0.940 for corny9 and corny8), and
the record specimen is mid-pack at 0.968. The tall batch-4 family
reaches an index as high as 0.986 at a transmissibility near unity,
so the front remains two-dimensional.

\paragraph{Pre-registered review of the objective choices} We
pre-registered an adversarial, data-first review of the objective
choices, run with the analysis tool named in the Acknowledgment. The
review had to commit to its own objective set from the raw campaign
files before reading ours. Its findings temper the round-1 formulation
and set the round-3 revision. First, the rebound fraction $e_{\mathrm{reb}}$ rests on a ballistic
interpretation of the second contact event that has not yet been
validated against synchronized video (a restrained-versus-unrestrained
check is scheduled). Second, the apparent
$t_{180}$-versus-$E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ trade-off
is driven substantially by the single strongest attenuator (Spearman
$\rho = -0.39$, $p = 0.38$ across the reliable specimens). Third,
per-drop standard errors are much smaller than the
between-specimen variation, and therefore understate design-level
uncertainty. In response, we printed and tested the batch-3 plate three times,
which measured the print-to-print scatter directly
(Section~\ref{sec:results:batches}) and confirmed the understatement
at an order of magnitude in $t_{180}$. The
between-seat replication rule now gates every extreme, the rebound
fraction stays a candidate constraint pending its ballistic validation, and the
constant-printed-mass projection removed the mass channel the review
flagged. We report this trajectory because the loop is
the contribution, and revising objectives mid-campaign, with the
revision criteria stated in advance, is probably how such loops behave in
practice.

\paragraph{Mass as a confound and as a design variable} Holding
solid CAD mass constant did not hold printed grams constant. PLA
prints at partial infill while thin TPU cables print near solid, so
the seed specimens span \SIrange{18.50}{22.29}{g}, and among the
mapped seed specimens mass and $t_{180}$ are correlated
($r = 0.82$). Printed mass is itself induced by the geometry and is
strongly collinear with member dimensions (light specimens \emph{are}
the thick-strut, thin-cable corner by construction), so the seed
observations cannot identify separate geometry and mass effects. A
post hoc mass-adjusted regression would be unstable and would
estimate a different, direct-effect quantity. We addressed the
confound by experimental design rather than adjustment. We used the
calibrated printed-mass model (Supplementary Information) to compare
geometries at fixed printed grams in batches 3 and 4, where the batch
mass CVs were 1.7 and 2.3\%. Over all 53 tested specimens, mass and transmissibility are
nearly uncorrelated ($r = 0.12$). The confound was broken by
construction, and $E_{\mathrm{reb}}$ keeps grams penalized where they
matter physically, in the motion returned to the payload.

\paragraph{Relation to prior art, and why simulation sits outside
the loop} Relative to Pajunen
et~al.~\cite{pajunen2019design}, we vary the geometry
under an optimization loop rather than characterizing a single
fabrication condition. Relative to Mo
et~al.~\cite{mo2023accelerated}, the loop's ground truth is physical
measurement, and no simulated number enters the objectives, the
acquisition, or candidate priority. We based that exclusion on an
exploratory multi-engine simulation
study conducted alongside the campaign (rigid-body through
finite-element fidelities, archived in the project repository). The
study could not reproduce the amplification that dominates the measured record,
predicted design-invariant rebound
where the measurement discriminates strongly, and correlated only
weakly with the measured transmissibility. Its rigid-strut models cap
the transmissibility near unity, while most printed specimens exceed it.
Its constant-mass
fairness constraints and its diagnosis of the seed batch's
mass confound did shape the campaign design, which is the
screening role we consider simulation to have earned here. Before
simulation takes on a larger role, it must demonstrate prospective
predictive skill on specimens it has not seen. The measured near-unity
transmission of most seed specimens also cautions against reading the
printed-tensegrity literature's attenuation results as generic. The
as-printed cells here are not pretensioned, and activating genuine
pretension in additively manufactured tensegrity (for example through
shrink-on-treatment routes) is an open direction we deliberately
scope out of the present campaign.

\paragraph{Practical considerations and path forward} PLA/TPU
interface durability under repeated impact remains open. No joint
failed during the repeated-drop measurement sessions, which covered more than
1,400 stabilized captures across 35 specimens, plus the second and
third prints of batch 3. Because
those sessions were not designed as controlled fatigue tests, the
observation does not establish interface durability. Pull-out
and fatigue characterization of the internal-anchor joint is
planned follow-on work. Sensitivity to
print-process covariates (the print key logs defects, humidity, and
the mid-batch nozzle change for this purpose) and transfer to
other material pairs likewise remain open. In the future we plan
the re-seat confirmations of the batch-4 attenuators, the replicate
rank-stability study of Section~\ref{sec:methods:rankstability}, the
metal-analog rank-transfer comparison (Supplementary Information,
Section~\siref{si:metal-validation}), quasi-static testing as an
extension of the characterization
(Section~\ref{sec:methods:testing}), pretension activation, tiled
multi-cell absorbers and lattices~\citep{sabounizawadzka2024experimental},
and miniaturization to crutch-tip envelopes. Long-term crutch use is
associated with substantial upper-extremity overuse injury and
ground-reaction-force impulses~\citep{requejo2005upperextremitykinetics,
rasouli2020walkingassistanceusing, manocha2021injuriesassociatedwith,
brasilbarrosdasilva2022painmappingand,
dozono2015peripheralneuropathiesin}. Existing
spring-loaded~\citep{segura2007mechanicsofambulation,
zhang2011biomechanicalevaluationof} and
polymer-damped~\citep{macgillivray2016theinfluenceof} tip designs
reduce loading rate but not peak transmitted force. Recent
tensegrity crutch concepts~\citep{gu2026tensegritycrutches} and
open-source 3D-printed crutch
work~\citep{mottaghi2025opensource3dprintable} suggest a plausible
translation path, with adjacent footwear and orthotic lattice
studies~\citep{wang2025porouslatticestructure,
zhang2024pressurereducingdesignof,
janarthanan2024additivemanufacturingof} providing transferable design
heuristics.

\paragraph{Limitations} The measured evidence base is 53 specimens: 35
across the four batches and 18 re-prints of the nine batch-3 designs,
which are the only designs tested more than once. The surrogate fit
includes the nine second prints but not yet the nine third prints.
That replication exposed the sensitivity to seating and printing, so
every single-specimen extreme, including the batch-4 record, is
provisional until its re-seat. The drop protocol probes one height,
one impact surface, and axial loading only. The specimens never leave
the elastic regime, so crush-energy metrics await the quasi-static
campaign. Per-specimen energy absorption in joules is also not
recoverable from the current rig, because the falling-mass and displacement
channels required for a force integral are not instrumented. The four
filament axes carry one value per batch, so their effects are not
identifiable. The
held-out prediction skill of the surrogate declined as the input space
grew (Section~\ref{sec:results:audits}). One tested seed specimen lacks
a design mapping, one seed design's official specimen assignment
carries a recorded discrepancy, and two seed designs have no session
record. All results come from a single printer, material pair, and
cell topology. We plan to address these limits with the replication
rule, the re-seat confirmations, the replicate rank-stability study
(Section~\ref{sec:methods:rankstability}), and the metal-analog
protocol (Supplementary Information,
Section~\siref{si:metal-validation}).

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}
% =============================================================================

We have presented an experiment-driven Bayesian-optimization
framework for designing multi-material 3D-printed tensegrity-inspired
energy absorbers, with TPU used in place of the traditional tension
elements and PLA members carrying compression most of the time. We
have run it through the recommend, fabricate, measure, and update
cycle three times on physical hardware. The framework parameterizes a
$T_3$-prism family (five geometry variables, joined in batch 3 by
infill and filament process variables) under a constant-mass fairness
projection. It fabricates each candidate in a single multi-material
FDM build with TPU tendons anchored inside the PLA strut ends and
characterizes every specimen with repeated instrumented drops (101
stabilized captures in the seed batch, about twenty thereafter). A
fully Bayesian Gaussian-process
surrogate~\citep{balandat2020botorch, eriksson2021saasbo} fitted to
those measurements recommends the next batch. Across the four measured batches (35
specimens), the transmissibility spanned 0.803 to 1.334, and most
specimens amplified the peak shock. The model-recommended batches grew
the dominated hypervolume 65\% over the Sobol seed front, ending with
a front whose five members are all model-chosen. The batch-4
best-predicted design set the program record at $t_{180} = 0.803$,
ten percent below the best seed specimen, and awaits its re-seat
confirmation under the campaign's replication rule. Printing one
batch's nine designs three times put the print-to-print standard
deviation of the transmissibility at 0.009, an order of magnitude
above the standard error of a session mean. A broadband seat gauge
flagged both of the largest single-print errors, so a single-print
extreme needs a replicate, or at least a clean re-seat, before it
counts.

The workflow is a candidate for other experimentally evaluated
architected structures whose constitutive response is difficult to
calibrate from first principles, but transfer beyond this printer,
material pair, topology, and axial impact condition remains untested.
